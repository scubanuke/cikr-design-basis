The Losses of Radar and Communications at the Philadelphia Approach Control Facility, 2025 to 2026

Event date: 28 April 2025, 9 May 2025, and 21 September 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

Since July 2024, the controllers who guide aircraft into Newark have worked from the Philadelphia approach control facility, while the system that processes their radar stayed in New York, linked by telecommunications circuits. Twice in the spring of 2025 those links failed for about ninety seconds, and controllers lost radar and radio contact with the aircraft they were guiding; Newark's operations have been limited since. On 21 September 2026 a primary circuit failed and its backup was found cut, ground stops held five airports in the region for most of a day, and about 7,000 flights were affected.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act. It is a series of accidental losses of a control facility's links, through old equipment and an accidental cut, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, The derating beside the outages (cut to a note). Newark's limits, set on the agency's stated basis of staffing, should be recorded beside the outages they followed, with the stress of a loss of the links named among their possible causes.
  • F-2, The independence that failed was in time. The backup's loss was found only on switching to it, so independence between primary and backup must include how soon a lost backup becomes known.
  • F-3, The transfer of airspace created the dependence. Moving the controllers away from their radar processing should have shown, before it took effect, that every path they would depend on was independent.
  • F-4, Withdrawn. Merged into F-5.
  • F-5, What the record does not show: how restoration ended and how the aircraft were reached. The record does not say when both paths were restored or how contact with the aircraft was regained, and restoration should be counted to all three of its end points.
  • F-6, Withdrawn. Its correction went to the facility class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of control facilities whose people see and speak through circuits they do not own: approach and enroute control in aviation and, in the other modes the class covers, rail dispatch and train control, vessel traffic services, and transit control centers. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose operators sit in one place while the systems they watch are processed in another. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know Your Paths as Built

The agency’s own steps after the losses of 2025 are its published actions: on 7 May 2025 it announced new high-bandwidth links between the cities, the replacement of copper with fiber, a temporary backup system, and a radar-processing hub at Philadelphia, and on 3 July 2025 it said it had moved to a new fiber network with two separate communications paths, keeping the temporary satellite system in place for now. On 21 September 2026 the primary failed and the backup was found cut on the same morning, so two paths announced were not two paths that held. This step is this instrument’s, from F-2: for each control site, list the route, the carrier, and the equipment of every path its data and radios use, mark what the paths share, and confirm the separation as built. Know the age of each circuit and replace the oldest ahead of failure, and arrange to learn of work planned near the routes before it begins.

A.3.2 Know When the Backup Is Gone

The agency’s order on facility operations already provides an alert for the loss of redundancy (FAA Order JO 1900.47G, ¶3-2). This step is this instrument’s, from F-2: set a time within which you learn that a backup has been lost, whether by the carrier’s report or by your own monitoring, and treat that loss as an event in its own right, acted on when it happens rather than found when the primary fails.

A.3.3 Check the Links Before You Move the Work

This step is this instrument’s, from F-3. The transfer of July 2024 was made for staffing, and it moved the controllers away from the system that processes their radar. Before any move of work between sites, or of territory between control centers, show the independence of every path on which the moved work will depend, and record a move made without that showing as a reduction in your margin.

A.3.4 Practice Without the Links, and Count the Whole Recovery

This step is this instrument’s, from F-5, with the note of F-1. Practice the case where screens and radios are lost with movements in progress, and after any real loss, record how everyone in motion was reached and how long it took. Count recovery to its last end point: the end of the stop, the return to full capacity, and the restoration of both paths. If operations run reduced afterward, record that beside the loss with its stated reason, and plan for the people on watch: on 5 May 2025 the agency said that some of the facility’s controllers had taken time off to recover from the stress of the outages.

A.4 For the Board

Since July 2024, the controllers who guide aircraft into Newark have worked from Philadelphia, while the system that processes their radar stayed in New York, so that their picture of the sky arrives over telecommunications lines between the two cities. In April and May 2025 those lines failed twice, for about ninety seconds each time, and controllers lost radar and radio contact with the aircraft they were guiding; some took leave afterward, and Newark has operated under limits since. On 21 September 2026 an aging circuit failed, and its backup, which had been cut that morning, was found cut when the system switched to it. Five airports in the region stopped traffic for most of a day, and about 7,000 flights were affected. No one suggests an attack; the record speaks of old equipment and an accidental cut. The lesson does not depend on the cause. A control room is only as good as the lines that feed it, two paths are only two if they are separate in route and in time, and a backup that is lost without anyone knowing is not a backup. Any operation whose people sit in one place and whose systems run in another has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
