The Nashville Bombing, December 2020

Event date: 25 December 2020 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

Early on Christmas morning 2020, a vehicle bomb exploded in the street in front of AT&T's central office in downtown Nashville. The building stood, but the blast cut its commercial power and disabled its generators, and its staff were kept out for about twelve hours while the scene was secured. The batteries ran out after about four to five hours, and telephone, wireless, internet, and 911 service failed across parts of Tennessee, Kentucky, and Alabama for days.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate act by an individual, not military action: the Federal Bureau of Investigation found that the bomber acted alone and that the act was not terrorism, and the record does not show the building was chosen for what it carried.

Findings

  • F-1, The building stood; its power plant did not. The structure and equipment survived, but the power connection and generators were lost from outside.
  • F-2, Endurance must outlast the exclusion. The batteries lasted about five hours, and staff were kept out for about twelve.
  • F-3, A central office's endurance against its own loss is its battery. With its generators gone, the building's endurance was its batteries alone.
  • F-4, One building's reach is counted in the map. The building carried other carriers' traffic, public-safety networks, and customers' backup paths, far beyond its own service.
  • F-5, Diversity is shown line by line and by test. Lines that 911 authorities believed independent ran through the same building.
  • F-6, Warning comes while the batteries run, on a path the failure does not cut. For hours before the failure, the 911 centers that depended on the building received no warning.
  • F-7, FirstNet and radio are second paths only where they are. Paths that shared nothing with the building carried; the public-safety network that shared its power did not.
  • F-8, The analog is a building chosen for what it carries. The design basis should consider a building chosen for the service it concentrates.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of telephone central offices, data centers, and other buildings that concentrate network service, and for the 911 centers, hospitals, airports, and agencies that depend on them. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose service depends on one building’s power. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Make Endurance Outlast the Exclusion

After the event the operator said it would extend its batteries at that building from about five hours to twelve or twenty-four and pursue access protocols with the Federal Bureau of Investigation and the Federal Communications Commission. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1, F-2, and F-3: protect a building’s power connection and generators against loss from outside it; size its endurance to outlast the time staff could be kept out after an incident, not only the time to start a generator; and agree in advance with law enforcement how you would enter, or act remotely, to preserve power at a damaged building that is a crime scene.

A.3.2 Know What One Building Carries, and Show Diversity by Test

Tennessee afterward required two physically diverse connections and a wireless connection for each 911 center. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-4, F-5, and F-7: map everything each building carries and for whom it is the only path; show the diversity of critical customers’ paths line by line and by test; and credit a second path, such as a public-safety network, only where it shares nothing with the building, its power included.

A.3.3 Warn While the Batteries Run

This step is this instrument’s, from F-6. When a building is running on batteries it cannot recharge, warn the 911 centers and other critical dependents at once, by a path the failure would not cut, so that they can move to their alternatives before the service fails.

A.4 For the Board

Early on Christmas morning 2020, a vehicle bomb exploded in the street in front of AT&T’s central office in downtown Nashville. The building stood and its equipment kept working on batteries, but the blast cut its commercial power and disabled its generators, and its staff were kept out for about twelve hours while the scene was secured. The batteries ran out after about four to five hours, and telephone, wireless, internet, and 911 service failed across parts of Tennessee, Kentucky, and Alabama; flights at Nashville stopped, and hospitals and payments were affected. Many 911 centers had no warning, and some found that lines they believed independent ran through the same building. The Federal Bureau of Investigation found that the bomber acted alone and that the act was not terrorism. The lessons are that a building that survives can still lose its service, that its backup power must outlast the time its staff can be kept out, and that one building may carry far more than its owner has counted. Any operation that concentrates service in one building has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
