The Defrost Attacks on Supermarket Refrigeration, October and November 2026

Event date: 11 and 12 October and 1 and 2 November 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.6 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

On the night of 11 October 2026, refrigerated cases and freezers in about 700 French supermarkets were set into defrost through remotely managed controllers reachable from the internet, many past their manufacturers' support, and temperatures rose to as much as 50°C. A second wave struck stores in several more countries on 1 and 2 November. Stock was discarded, equipment was damaged, and in some stores the heaters started fires; no shortage or illness was reported.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate and repeated attack, not placed at the military-action tier: no government has attributed it to a state, it came in peacetime, and it removed stock rather than supply. The European Union's statement is recorded as one of intent, not attribution.

Findings

  • F-1, A distributed tier is concentrated by what its facilities share. Hundreds of stores failed in one night because they ran the same controllers.
  • F-2, The cold chain's protective function is independent of the controller it protects. The controller the attackers held also held the limit, the alarm, and the display.
  • F-3, Temperature abuse is a safety-axis event. The controllers can be made to hide a warming as well as cause one.
  • F-4, Building equipment driven by a digital command is a fire initiator in occupied retail space. Defrost heaters run without limit started fires in stores.
  • F-5, Equipment past its support is a design-basis condition. Most of the controllers named receive no updates.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for supermarkets, food retailers, and cold-storage operators, for the refrigeration contractors who manage their controllers remotely, and for any operator of building equipment a contractor can reach over a network. It recasts the interim actions of v0.4 into the three-part form, carrying each of them into A.3. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Remove the Exposure, Including the Routes Around It

The French government’s computer emergency response team told operators to take affected units, and any unit reachable at a public address, off the internet; to restore remote access only through a secured private network or protected connection; to treat filtering by source address as a short-term measure only; and to check insurance conditions before cutting a refrigeration contractor’s data link. Coop’s report that some of its fridges sat on a 4G mobile link shows a route the alert does not name. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-1: treat every path to the controller, the contractor’s and any cellular one included, as part of the exposure, and count the stores that share one make or one contractor as one exposure.

A.3.2 Patch, Harden, and Reset

The alert told operators to update firmware, set Danfoss devices to their strict security mode, change default passwords, review the whole configuration and especially the defrost schedules, and, where a unit is compromised, reboot it, as Danfoss advises, or reset it to factory settings and reconfigure it before returning it to service, a reset alone not sufficing if the unit stays exposed or unpatched. Danfoss fixed the flaws in the supported AK-SM 800A at firmware 4.2 and 4.3.1, installed by hand, and Sabcobel’s account of the second wave in Belgium is the evidence that patching after the first reduced the harm.

A.3.3 Retire or Isolate Unsupported Units

Danfoss declared the AK-SC 355 end of life in 2016 and the AK-SM 800 in 2021, and has long advised migration; the alert states that keeping an unpatched end-of-life device exposed carries a very high probability of compromise. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-5: replace or isolate each such unit, and set the date by which it is done.

A.3.4 Put a Limit on the Heat the Controller Cannot Override

No authority has yet published this action; it is this instrument’s, from F-2 and F-4. Fit a thermostat or high-temperature cutout on the case or cold room itself, acting on its own measurement, that ends heating and raises an alarm whatever the supervisory controller commands. It bounds both the spoiled stock and the fire.

A.3.5 Report and Coordinate

The alert asks that compromises be reported to the national computer emergency response team. Sweden’s national cybersecurity center is responding to incidents, and British retailers are working with the National Cyber Security Centre to secure their refrigeration. This instrument adds no step of its own here.

A.3.6 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the units on this record were reached at public addresses, and some sat behind a contractor’s link or on a 4G mobile link, so a store’s own address space may not hold them. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on elements A-1 and A-2 and F-1.

A.4 For the Board

On the night of 11 October 2026, refrigerated cases and freezers in about 700 French supermarkets, of every chain, were set into defrost through their remotely managed controllers, and temperatures rose to as much as 50°C within hours. On the night of 1 November it happened again in several more countries. The controllers, of several makes and many past their manufacturers’ support, were reachable from the internet, directly, through contractors, or over cellular links on the units. Stock was discarded, equipment damaged, and in some French stores the heaters started fires that the sprinklers answered; where alarms sounded, the food was already spoiled. No shortage and no illness were reported. No government has attributed the attacks to a state; the European Union called the second wave a deliberate attempt to disrupt the food supply chain, which is its statement of intent, not an attribution. The lessons are that stores sharing one make of controller or one contractor share one exposure, that every path to a controller must be closed, and that a limit on the heat must act on the case itself whatever the controller commands. Any operator of remotely managed building equipment has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
