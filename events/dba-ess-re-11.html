The Shooting at the Route 91 Harvest Festival, October 2017

Event date: 1 October 2017 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

At 10:05 p.m. on 1 October 2017, a gunman firing from the thirty-second floor of a hotel in Las Vegas fired into a crowd of more than 22,000 at the Route 91 Harvest festival for about ten minutes, killing fifty-eight people that night. Thirteen agencies, several hundred police officers, and about 130 private ambulances responded, many without assignment, and about 800 patients reached hospitals by their own means. Because the gunman's position was not located for about an hour, fire and medical crews waited to enter, and command vetted sixteen reports of attacks elsewhere before sending anyone.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate attack, not military action: the gunman aimed at the crowd, not at the response, and nothing shows the response forces were chosen or studied.

Findings

  • F-1, Capacity was abundant and unaccounted. Command could not direct units it did not know were there.
  • F-2, The class's account of the false reports needs correction. The reports of other attacks were vetted, and no source shows units drawn to false scenes.
  • F-3, Self-dispatch is to be absorbed, not forbidden. Several hundred officers and dozens of ambulances arrived without assignment, and much of their work saved lives.
  • F-4, The patient-transfer line presumes a transfer. About 800 patients reached hospitals without a transfer from a crew.
  • F-5, A planned gathering is a known concentration. The festival's plan left out the fire service, and its medical supplies were sized for an ordinary night.
  • F-6, The entry rule is needed outside military-action conditions. Crews faced the decision to enter a scene with an unlocated threat.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its forward reasoning about deliberate misdirection went to Project 9 as use-case material.
  • F-8, Common accountability failed before any network did. No network failed, and accountability across agencies still broke down.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the police, fire, and emergency medical agencies of an area, the 911 centers that dispatch them, and the private ambulance companies an area’s plan relies on. It is also for the organizers and venues of large planned gatherings, whose obligations the Commercial Facilities series states. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Plan Planned Gatherings, and Absorb Self-Dispatch

The 1 October After-Action Report, prepared by the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s National Exercise Division with the Clark County Fire Department and the Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, recommends that agencies recognize that off-duty personnel will respond and bring them to staging. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-3, and F-5: count a unit as available only when command knows its location and assignment; set check-in points on every approach, known in advance to every agency and to off-duty personnel; treat private ambulance companies the plan relies on as part of command from the start; and for a planned gathering above a size the area sets, join every discipline in the plan, staff a dispatch position for the event, and size medical supplies at the venue for a surge.

A.3.2 State the Entry Rule, and Vet Reports Before Committing

The same report found that the unified commanders acted appropriately in vetting reports of other attacks before committing resources. This step is the instrument’s own, from F-6: state the rule for entering a scene where a threat is continuing or unlocated for any deliberate event, not only for war, with the delay it accepts decided in advance.

A.3.3 Track Patients, and Exercise Accountability Under Convergence

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4 and F-8. Plan for patients who reach hospitals without a transfer from a crew, so that the distribution of patients is known; and exercise common accountability and mutual-aid channels under the convergence of many units, not only under the loss of links.

A.4 For the Board

At 10:05 p.m. on 1 October 2017, a gunman on the thirty-second floor of a hotel in Las Vegas fired into a crowd of more than 22,000 at the Route 91 Harvest festival for about ten minutes, killing fifty-eight people that night. The response was large and fast, thirteen agencies, several hundred police officers, and about 130 private ambulances, but command could not see much of it: many officers and ambulances arrived without assignment, about 800 patients reached hospitals by their own means, and the plan for the festival had not included the fire service. Because the gunman’s position was not located for about an hour, fire and medical crews waited to enter, and command vetted sixteen reports of attacks elsewhere before sending anyone. The lessons are that a large planned gathering is a known concentration and should be planned by every discipline that would answer it, that help which command cannot track is not yet capacity, and that the rule for entering a scene with an unlocated threat must be stated in advance. Any area that hosts large gatherings has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this area does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
