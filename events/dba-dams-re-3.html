The Failures of the Edenville and Sanford Dams, May 2020

Event date: 19 May 2020 · Read from instrument version 0.3 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

On 19 May 2020, after heavy rain, the Edenville Dam on Michigan's Tittabawassee River failed, and its flood overwhelmed the Sanford Dam downstream, which failed in turn. Edenville's federal license had been revoked in 2018 over its spillway capacity, and its regulation had passed to the state. About 11,000 people were evacuated and about 1,000 structures were severely damaged or destroyed; no one died.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: an embankment failed at a high pool, and the flood took the dam below it.

Findings

  • F-1, The cascade's analysis and warning survive a change of regulator. The four dams in series were not analyzed and warned as one.
  • F-2, The downstream authority warns on its own trigger. The county ordered evacuation before the owner activated its plan.
  • F-3, A gate is credited only as far as people can safely work it. Edenville's gates could be opened only part of the way.
  • F-4, A dam's record moves with its regulation. The state received little information about the dam when regulation passed to it.
  • F-5, An event's footprint is recorded for the model. The flood's extent was not recorded below the confluence.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for owners of dams in a series on one river, the state and federal regulators that oversee them, and the county emergency managers and facilities in the valley below. It is also for any owner whose dam changes regulator or ownership. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Analyze and Warn the Cascade as One

The independent forensic team found the failures foreseeable and preventable, the result of decisions and inactions over the dams’ history by the owners, regulators, and others. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1 and F-4: keep one analysis and one warning for a cascade, surviving any change of regulator; and move a dam’s whole record with its regulation.

A.3.2 Credit Only the Gate People Can Safely Work

This step is this instrument’s, from F-3. Credit a gate’s capacity only as far as people can safely open it in the conditions of a flood.

A.3.3 Let the County Act, and Record the Flood

Midland County ordered evacuation about midnight, before the owners activated their emergency plan, and the incident response review calls for data collected before an event to support inundation modeling. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-2 and F-5: give the downstream authority its own trigger; prepare facilities in the footprint to shut down ahead of a flood; and record the flood’s observed extent.

A.4 For the Board

On 19 May 2020, after heavy rain, the Edenville Dam on Michigan’s Tittabawassee River failed and its flood overwhelmed the Sanford Dam downstream, which failed in turn. The two were among four dams of one owner built in series in the 1920s. Edenville’s federal license had been revoked in 2018 because its spillways could not pass the largest flood its design required, and regulation passed to the state, which had little information about the dam. Its gates could be opened only part of the way. About 11,000 people were evacuated, the county having ordered evacuation before the owner activated the emergency plan; about 1,000 structures were severely damaged or destroyed, and no one died. A chemical plant downstream shut down ahead of the flood. The independent forensic team, whose report is the record, found the failures foreseeable and preventable. The lessons are that a cascade of dams must be analyzed and warned as one, that a gate counts only as far as people can safely open it, and that a dam’s record must follow it when its regulator changes. Any owner of a dam in a series has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this dam does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
