The Overfilling and Breach of the Taum Sauk Upper Reservoir, December 2005

Event date: 14 December 2005, read to the Commission's order of 2 October 2006 (record closed) · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Industry and Economy collection

Impacted sector: electric

What happened

Before dawn on 14 December 2005 the upper reservoir of the Taum Sauk pumped-storage project in Missouri, which had no spillway and was run from a control room more than a hundred miles away, was pumped over its parapet. Its three level transducers hung on one support that had come loose and read about two feet low; the backup probes had been raised after an overtopping in September, their alarm hung on one probe, and a programming error had disconnected them from one pump. The remote operator did not know the backup was ineffective, and the regulator had not been told. The rockfill crest eroded, the reservoir emptied in minutes through Johnson's Shut-Ins State Park, and the park superintendent's family was swept away in their house and survived.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: an overfill on instruments that read wrong and a protection changed without anyone deciding the fill knowing. None of the four characteristics is present.

Findings

  • F-1, The reading was adjusted to look right. Any offset to a reading or change to a protection is a governed act, recorded and tested.
  • F-2, The person deciding the fill did not know the protection was gone. Show the protection's state to whoever decides the fill.
  • F-3, The backup had never been shown to work. Test the protection end to end, as installed and after every change.
  • F-4, Nothing passive stood between the pumps and the crest. A reservoir filled by pumps carries an overflow sized to them, or declares its absence.
  • F-5, The precursor did not reach the regulator. Report an overtopping or a protection found out of service, and restrict the fill until it is shown in service.
  • F-6, Erratum: why the probes were too high. They were moved on 1 October 2005, on the Commission's order.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owners and operators of pumped-storage projects and of any reservoir filled or drawn by pumps or gates under remote control, for the contractors who maintain their instruments and controls, and for the dam-safety regulators who oversee them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility classes; this annex is addressed to a reservoir. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an owner answers about its own reservoir (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an owner can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s letter of 13 January 2006 required every pumped-storage licensee to review the effectiveness of its instrumentation and monitoring, its operating procedures, a fault-tree analysis, its operator training, and its Emergency Action Plan. The over-pumping protection criteria presented to the Commission’s pumped-storage meeting of November 2006 call for a protection separate from level control and held to stricter requirements, fail-safe, hardwired so that its shutdown does not pass through computers, controllers, or timers, and tested periodically, and warn that redundancy without independence gives a false sense of security. The first is the Commission’s; the second is its consultant’s, and this instrument does not find that it became a requirement. No step of this instrument’s is added here.

A.3.2 Govern Every Change, Show the Protection, and Give the Water a Way Out

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 to F-5. Treat any offset, recalibration, or change to a level reading or a high-level protection as a change to the protection, approved, recorded, and followed by a test. Test the protection from the water at its level to the trip of every pump, as installed and after every change. Show the person who decides the fill whether the protection is in service, and do not fill near the top while it is not. If the reservoir is filled by pumps and has no overflow that can pass what they deliver, say so in its safety case, with the date the condition ends. And report an overtopping, or a protection found out of service, to the regulator before the next fill.

A.4 For the Board

Before dawn on 14 December 2005, the upper reservoir of a pumped-storage plant in the Missouri Ozarks was pumped over the top of its wall, and the wall gave way. A billion gallons and more ran down the mountain in minutes, through a state park; the park superintendent’s family was swept away in their house and survived. The reservoir had no spillway. Its level gauges had come loose from their mounts the year before, all three together, and read about two feet low. The backup sensors meant to stop the pumps had been raised after a smaller overflow in September, and a programming error had disconnected them from one pump. The operator running the plant from a hundred miles away did not know the backup was not working, and the regulator was not told of the September overflow or the changes until after the breach. The company paid the largest penalty the federal regulator had then imposed in a hydroelectric case and rebuilt the reservoir with an overflow. The lessons are that three gauges on one mount are one gauge, that a reading adjusted until it looks right is not a reading shown to be right, that a backup nobody has tested is not a backup, and that the person deciding must know when the protection is gone. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own reservoirs, and most of the answers cost little to find.
