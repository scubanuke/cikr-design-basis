The Attack on Kyivstar, December 2023

Event date: 12 December 2023 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 12 December 2023, an adversary that had been inside the network of Kyivstar, Ukraine's largest mobile operator, for months destroyed systems on which its core ran and was managed, and some twenty-four million subscribers lost mobile and internet service. National roaming did not work because the lost core could not pass subscriber information to the other networks, and air-raid alerts and payment terminals failed in some regions. With vendor help, service came back in stages over about eight days.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Military action: the Security Service of Ukraine attributes the attack to Sandworm, which it identifies as a unit of Russia's military intelligence service, and that attribution is stated as its own. The adversary was inside for months and chose its moment against the country's largest carrier.

Findings

  • F-1, The core's management does not share the enterprise's identity. Entry came through a compromised employee account, and the core's management should be reachable only by separate identities.
  • F-2, The virtualization layer is a surface. What was destroyed was the platform the core ran on, whose loss takes every function it hosts.
  • F-3, Isolating the core is a planned act. The network was shut down to limit the damage, and that decision should be planned in advance.
  • F-4, Vendor help is part of the means of restoration. About a thousand people and three vendors restored the core in about eight days.
  • F-5, A fallback among networks is designed for the lost core. National roaming failed because it depended on the core that was lost.
  • F-6, Alerting and payments fail with a carrier's core. Air-raid alerts and payment terminals failed where they depended on the carrier.
  • F-7, The residual the class cites is worse than Kyivstar. The core was restored from means that survived, so Kyivstar shows the edge of the worst case, not its limit.
  • F-8, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event was taken in by the class.
  • F-9, No block reaches emergency calls. Roaming was blocked to protect other networks, and the record does not show whether subscribers could still reach emergency services.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of mobile and other core networks, the governments and regulators that arrange fallbacks among them, and the alerting authorities, banks, and other dependents that rely on one carrier. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose operational core is reached through the same identities as its office systems. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Separate the Core’s Management From the Enterprise

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads; the Security Service of Ukraine’s account and the carrier’s are its record. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1: reach the core’s management only through identities and administrative paths that the enterprise systems do not share, and watch those paths for an adversary who has been inside for months.

A.3.2 Plan the Isolation and the Restoration

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2, F-3, F-4, and F-7. Count the virtualization and cloud platform under your core as one surface whose loss takes every function it hosts; make the isolation of the core a planned act, with its authority and the security services’ part stated in advance and its means off the core; and make your vendors’ help part of the means of restoration, by channels the adversary cannot reach. Kyivstar restored service in about eight days with about a thousand people and three vendors.

A.3.3 Design the Fallback for the Lost Core

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-5, F-6, and F-9. Design any fallback among networks, national roaming among them, to work when the home core is lost; make sure no block placed on that fallback to protect the other networks reaches emergency calls; and do not let alerting or payments depend on one carrier’s core.

A.4 For the Board

On 12 December 2023, an adversary that had been inside the network of Kyivstar, Ukraine’s largest mobile operator, for months destroyed systems on which its core ran and was managed, and some twenty-four million subscribers lost mobile and internet service. The Security Service of Ukraine attributed the attack to Sandworm, which it identifies as a unit of Russia’s military intelligence service; that attribution is its own. The carrier and the security services shut the whole network down to limit the damage. National roaming, meant to let subscribers use other networks, did not work, because the lost core could not pass subscriber information to them, and air-raid alerts and payment terminals failed in some regions. With about a thousand people and the help of three vendors, service came back in stages over about eight days. On the carrier’s account, the way in was a compromised employee account. The lessons are that the core must not share identities with the office, that its isolation and restoration must be planned before the day, and that a fallback among networks must work when the home core is gone. Any operation whose core can be reached through its office systems has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
