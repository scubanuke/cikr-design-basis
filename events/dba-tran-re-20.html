Nationwide Ground Stop of 13 November 2026

Event date: 13 November 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On the afternoon of 13 November 2026, the network that carries radar and communications data to the Federal Aviation Administration's air traffic control facilities became unstable, and the agency stopped every departure in the United States for about four hours and twenty minutes. On the Secretary of Transportation's account, a router being installed during routine maintenance at one remote site flooded other routers with connection attempts, and within about forty-five minutes 17 of the 22 enroute centers were affected. About 4,000 aircraft already in the air landed, nearly 5,000 flights were cancelled that day, and officials said there was no sign of a cyberattack; the investigation continues.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act on the record. It is an accidental loss of the links of most of the country's enroute centers at once, below the threshold for the military-action tier. The determination is provisional, because no official cause has been published and the investigation has not ruled out an attack.

Findings

  • F-1, Independence across facilities. One network carried every center's links, so when it failed no center was left to take over another's airspace.
  • F-2, The governed change stops one layer too high. Changes to the network that many facilities share, including maintenance, modernization, and security patches, are not governed the way changes to shared information systems already are.
  • F-3, The reach of the protective stop. Stopping every departure was the right call, but the design basis does not bound how much of the nation's movement one loss of confidence may stop, or for how long.
  • F-4, What the record does not show: contact with the aircraft aloft. The record does not yet show how controllers kept contact with the aircraft in the air or handed them between centers.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of control facilities and of the networks that connect them, in aviation and in the other modes the class covers: rail dispatch and train control, vessel traffic services and transit control centers. It is also for any owner whose sites share one network, in any sector. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know What Your Sites Share

No authority has yet published an action from this event; this step is this instrument’s, from F-1. List, for each control site and for its backup site, the network, the carrier and the equipment by which its radios, surveillance and data arrive, and mark what every site has in common. Whatever is on every line is what one event can take from all of them.

A.3.2 Govern Changes to the Shared Network

This step is this instrument’s, from F-2. A change to a network that carries more than one site’s links should be approved as a change to every site it reaches, tried on a part of the network first, held there long enough to show it works, and only then spread, with a way back. Maintenance and modernization are such changes, and so is a patch: on the Transportation Secretary’s account, a router installed during routine maintenance at one site unsettled 17 of 22 enroute centers on 13 November 2026. The agency’s own rule for its notice system after January 2023, which the class records at §4.3, is the model; this event suggests that it applies one layer down.

A.3.3 Practice Without the Network

The class’s communications requirements ask that sites be able to speak to the people moving and to each other over means that share nothing with the primary network, and that this be exercised with the network assumed lost (CDB-3, CDB-4, CDB-5, CDB-7). Practice the case where every site loses the network at once, not only where one does.

A.3.4 Design How Much You Will Have to Stop

This step is this instrument’s, from F-3. The agency’s stop was the safe choice, and an operator should make the same choice when its data cannot be trusted. When the question is safety, cost does not enter the decision on the day. Cost belongs in the design, before the day: look for a cost-effective way to be just as safe, so that a loss confined to part of the network can be met by a stop confined to part of the operation, safely, and not the whole of it.

A.4 For the Board

On the afternoon of 13 November 2026, the network that connects the country’s air traffic control centers failed. Officials said that a router being installed during routine maintenance at one remote site flooded the network with connection attempts, and older routers began giving inconsistent data; 17 of the 22 high-altitude centers were affected. The agency did the safe thing: it stopped every departure in the country rather than move aircraft on information it could not trust, and about 4,000 aircraft already in the air landed safely. The stop lasted over four hours and nearly 5,000 flights were cancelled. Officials said there was no sign of a cyberattack, and the investigation continues. The lesson does not depend on the cause. Every center depended on the same network, so when it failed there was no center left to take over, and one change to that network, made to improve it, reached most of them at once. Any operation whose sites share one network has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
