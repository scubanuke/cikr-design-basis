The Failure of Coordination in Hurricane Katrina, 2005

Event date: 26 August to September 2005 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

Hurricane Katrina made landfall on 29 August 2005, after a warning found adequate days in advance, and its surge breached the levees and floodwalls of New Orleans. The storm flooded the city's operations center, the police headquarters, and the National Guard's headquarters, and some parishes could not reach the state for eleven days. Breaches were reported from the morning of landfall, but that evening the federal picture said the levees had not been breached, and the federal government ran three commands.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: the hazard was a hurricane and the failure of engineered flood defenses, though the storm took out operations centers and the links between them as an adversary might aim to.

Findings

  • F-1, For a forecast hazard, the point of unified command is keyed to the forecast. The full federal response was engaged the day after landfall, on requests the storm had cut off.
  • F-2, Each part of an action has one holder, named before the incident. Three federal commands ran at once, and the Guard and the police each said the other had the lead.
  • F-3, A fused picture carries disagreement on a catastrophic fact. Breach reports were overridden by a reassuring account.
  • F-4, For a regional hazard, the backup stands outside the footprint and each tier can act alone. The city's centers were lost to one flood, while a neighboring parish's hardened center held.
  • F-5, Qualification is shown by exercise against the catastrophic case, and its lessons are closed. The city's office had three staff, and the lessons of an exercise for this storm were incompletely implemented.
  • F-6, Who holds military support tasks under each status is settled in the map. Command of military forces in the state was settled during the incident.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its corrections of two facility classes' accounts of the event went to those classes as errata.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for emergency management agencies at every tier, the operations centers they run, and the police, National Guard, and other forces that must act together in a disaster. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose response depends on coordination among organizations that may lose contact with each other. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Key Unified Command to the Forecast, and Name Each Holder

The House committee found that the federal Incident of National Significance should have been declared no later than 27 August 2005, two days before landfall, and that the Catastrophic Incident Annex, which would have let federal agencies move resources without waiting for requests, should have been invoked. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, and F-6: key unified command to the forecast of a catastrophic hazard; name one holder for each part of the response before the incident; and settle in advance who authorizes the tasks of military forces under each status in which they may serve.

A.3.2 Place the Backup Outside the Footprint, and Let Each Tier Act Alone

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4. For a regional hazard, place the backup operations center outside the event’s footprint, harden the primary, as Jefferson Parish’s held, and prepare each tier to act on its own when it cannot reach the others.

A.3.3 Carry Disagreement in the Picture, and Exercise Against the Catastrophe

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-5. Carry contradicting reports of a catastrophic fact in the shared picture until they are resolved; size staff to the catastrophic duration, qualify them by exercise against that event, and close the lessons of each exercise.

A.4 For the Board

Hurricane Katrina made landfall on 29 August 2005, after a warning the House committee found adequate fifty-six hours before. Its surge breached the levees and floodwalls of New Orleans, and most of the city flooded. The storm took the centers from which coordination was to be run: the city’s operations center, the police headquarters, and the National Guard’s headquarters were all flooded and abandoned, while the hardened center of a neighboring parish held. Some parishes could not reach the state for eleven days. Breaches were reported from the morning of landfall, but that evening the federal picture said the levees had not been breached. At the Superdome the Guard and the police each said the other had the lead, and the federal government ran three commands. Congressional committees found that the federal response waited on declarations and requests the storm had cut off. The lessons are that command for a forecast catastrophe must be set by the forecast, that backup centers must stand outside the hazard’s footprint, and that every part of the response must have one holder named before the event. Any operation that depends on many organizations acting together has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
