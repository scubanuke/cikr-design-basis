The Strike on the Okhmatdyt Children's Hospital, July 2024

Event date: 8 July 2024 · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

On 8 July 2024, during a mass missile attack on Ukraine, the national children's hospital in Kyiv was hit; the United Nations monitoring mission assessed a high likelihood of a direct hit, and the governments' statements on the weapon differ. Dialysis and intensive care were destroyed or badly damaged, a child was in surgery when the power failed, and 94 children were moved to other hospitals in the same city. The record is open.

Tier

Military action, tier not established. Military action, in an armed conflict during a mass missile attack, with each statement on the weapon its author's own.

Findings

  • F-1, The patient who cannot be moved is sheltered where the care is. Children on dialysis and ventilators were in the buildings destroyed.
  • F-2, A service that lives in one building is a single point. Dialysis and transplant services were lost with their buildings.
  • F-3, The receiving hospitals are under the same attack. Patients were moved to hospitals in the same city under the same campaign.
  • F-4, Power reaches the point of care after a strike. Power failed during surgery, and the elevators were out.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for hospitals that could be struck in a war, especially tertiary and national hospitals whose services no other hospital can replace, and for the health authorities that plan where their patients would go. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Shelter the Patient Who Cannot Be Moved, and Give Each Irreplaceable Service a Second Place

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2: give care that cannot be interrupted in protected space, or state the patients who cannot be protected in place with the warning time the region’s alerting gives; and give each service no other hospital can replace a second location or a declared residual.

A.3.2 Name Receiving Hospitals Beyond the Struck Area, and Keep Power at the Point of Care

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-4. Name receiving hospitals for each group of patients, one outside the area a campaign may strike together; plan discharge as a protective action; let staff and supplies move with their patients; and keep power and light at the point of care, with an evacuation route that needs no elevators.

A.4 For the Board

On 8 July 2024, during a mass missile attack on Ukraine, Okhmatdyt, the country’s national children’s hospital in Kyiv, was hit. The United Nations Human Rights Monitoring Mission assessed a high likelihood of a direct hit and factors suggesting a Russian missile, without making a determination; Ukraine’s Security Service said missile fragments were found and called it a war crime; Russia’s Ministry of Defence said the damage came from a Ukrainian air defense missile. Each statement is its author’s own, and the record is open. The toxicology building, where children received dialysis, was destroyed and intensive care was destroyed or badly damaged; a child was in surgery when the power failed, and patients were carried down stairs with the elevators out. Ninety-four children were moved to other hospitals in the same city, 465 were sent home, and national services such as transplants were lost for months. The counts of dead and wounded differ by publisher. The lessons are that care that cannot be interrupted must be given where it can be protected, that no irreplaceable service should live in one building, and that receiving hospitals must lie beyond the area struck. Any hospital that could be struck in a war has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this hospital is ready, and most of the answers cost little to find.
