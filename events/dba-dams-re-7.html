The Failure of the New Orleans Hurricane Protection System in Hurricane Katrina, August 2005

Event date: 29 August 2005 (record closed) · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Industry and Economy collection

Impacted sector: Healthcare and Public Health, Emergency Services

What happened

Katrina's surge exceeded the New Orleans system's design in places, and many reaches were overtopped and eroded. At four breaches on the 17th Street, London Avenue, and Inner Harbor Navigation canals, I-walls failed through their foundations before water reached their tops. Most of the city flooded for weeks. The task force found the system incomplete, inconsistent, and not performing as a system; the independent team traced the failures to engineering errors and the interactions among its federal and local keepers.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: a line failing to a storm. None of the four characteristics is present.

Findings

  • F-1, The Line Had Many Keepers and No Owner of Its Level. A line of many keepers has one owner of its level.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owners and keepers of levees, floodwalls, and flood gates, the communities they protect, and the agencies that build and inspect them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to a line of protection. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an owner answers about its own line (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an owner can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one owner. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the owner; it is that owner’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

The task force’s and the independent team’s reports set out the failures they found. The class’s requirements (§4.2, §4.3, §5.1) state the line as one system with its lowest reach as its level, floodwalls shown to hold to their tops, armored overtopping, and the evacuation margin. The National Levee Safety Program and the levee database kept by the Army Corps of Engineers with the Federal Emergency Management Agency set out related practice. Each is its author’s.

A.3.2 Make One Party Answer for the Whole Line

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1. Name one party accountable for the level of your whole line of protection, every reach, wall, gate, and pump, whoever built or keeps each part, and give it the authority to require each keeper to bring its reach up to that level.

A.4 For the Board

When Hurricane Katrina’s storm surge reached New Orleans on 29 August 2005, the system of levees and floodwalls meant to protect the city failed. In places the surge was higher than the system was designed for, but at four places floodwalls along the city’s canals collapsed before the water reached their tops, because their foundations gave way. Most of the city flooded and stayed flooded for weeks. Investigators found that the system had been built and kept by several agencies and boards, that its height and strength varied from reach to reach, and that no one held the whole system to one standard. The lessons are that a city is protected only as well as the weakest reach of its line, and that one party must answer for the whole of it. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own line, and most of the answers cost little to find.
