Hacktivist Attacks on Small Water Systems, 2024 to 2026

Event date: January 2024 to 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.6 · Lifelines collection

What happened

From January 2024, groups presenting as hacktivists attacked small water systems in Texas, Pennsylvania, Denmark, Poland, and Spain, overflowing a tank in Muleshoe, Texas, and in Tureby Alkestrup, Denmark, dropping pressure to zero and then bursting pipe by overpressure. Denmark and Poland state that the groups act for the Russian state; that is their attribution. No supply was lost for longer than a day.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A state-attributed campaign below the military-action threshold: no incident met the four characteristics.

Findings

  • F-1, The taxonomy has no place for a state acting through a hacktivist persona. Two governments say the groups act for a state, with the skill of hacktivists.
  • F-2, A state-attributed campaign below the threshold is a design-basis condition. The campaign was attributed to a state while no incident met the threshold.
  • F-3, Commanded hydraulic consequences belong in the cyber category. Remote commands drove pressure to zero and then burst pipe.
  • F-4, The coordination threshold waits for a confirmation the campaign is built to deny. The campaign was recognized only when governments aggregated the record.
  • F-5, The adversary's own publication is an indicator. The groups published claims and video of their attacks.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owner of a water or wastewater system, and especially a small one, and for the engineers who advise owners. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to a plant. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an owner answers about the owner’s own system (A.2); the actions the authorities have published, each stated as theirs, with this instrument’s own additions marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an owner can give a board or council (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one system. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the owner; it is that system’s finding, and the place to start. The questions fit within an AWWA J100 or America’s Water Infrastructure Act risk and resilience assessment and can be worked as a walkdown of the plant and its remote stations.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Take the Screens Off the Internet

The joint advisory tells operators to reduce the exposure of operational technology to the public internet; its account of method shows that the groups find operator interfaces by scanning for remote-screen connections and log in to whatever answers. ABW names direct internet exposure as one of the two routes into Poland’s plants. A remote-screen or remote-desktop connection that answers from the internet should be removed, or placed behind a private network with individually assigned, multi-factor sign-in. A cellular modem at a remote station is a route of its own.

A.3.2 Change Every Default and Weak Password

The joint advisory calls for robust authentication on operational assets; ABW names weak password policy as the other route. A controller on its maker’s password, or on a four-digit PIN like Tureby’s, should be changed now to one of sufficient length and complexity, and the change recorded. When buying a controller, its support for individual accounts and strong authentication is a purchasing criterion; Tureby’s committee bought on price.

A.3.3 Know What Can Be Reached and What It Can Command

The joint advisory calls for asset management that maps data flows and access points. The owner should know which screens can be reached from where, and what each can command. Network maps and asset-condition records belong in the same inventory: in an attacker’s hands they show where a pipe is weak, and they should be held where the control-system attacker cannot reach them, which is this instrument’s reading from Section 4.1. A limit the operator interface can override is not a limit against an attacker; that reading is this instrument’s, from F-3.

A.3.4 Put the Last Protection in the Field

No authority on this record has published this action; it is this instrument’s, from F-3, and it is the one step that bounds the consequence whatever the attacker holds. It differs by mechanism (Section 4.1). Against sustained overpressure, the strongest protection is pumps whose pressure at full speed stays below what the weakest pipe can carry as it is today, so that no command can break it; where that is not so, a speed limit set in the drive, a high-pressure trip at the pump and relief sized for steady flow. Against water hammer, closing-speed limits set in the valve actuators, controlled pump stopping, and transient protection such as anticipator valves or air chambers where the network needs it; relief sized for steady pressure may be too slow for a pressure wave. Against overfilling, a hardwired high-level cutoff on each tank; against a runaway chemical feed, limits at the pump. Each acts on its own measurement, and a local gauge or level indication the network cannot alter gives the operator a reading to trust. The step is to confirm that none of these can be changed from the screen, and that their settings reflect the condition of what they protect: an old or corroded main bursts at a pressure a new one carries, so protection set for the network as designed may not hold the network as it is. Asset condition remains a factor in whether anything fails, engineering controls or not, and belongs in the same walkdown.

A.3.5 Keep the Hands-On Skill

Where control was lost on this record, the utility restored service by hand, and Hale Center ran by hand while its system was disconnected. Framework §5.2 asks for a manual procedure for every control function and practice in it. The procedure should say how long the system can be run by hand with the staff the utility actually has, around the clock, before people, chemicals or fuel run out; an outage of hours is one problem and an outage of weeks is another.

A.3.6 Report, and Verify Before Answering a Claim

Report an unexplained control change to the state primacy agency, to CISA and the FBI, and to WaterISAC, and say whether a campaign was known to be active. If a group claims an attack on the utility, check the control system’s records before confirming or denying it; the groups publish true and false claims alike. That step is this instrument’s, from F-5.

A.3.7 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the groups on this record found operator screens by scanning for remote-screen connections, and a screen behind a cellular modem at a remote station answers at the carrier’s address, not the utility’s. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element A-1.

A.4 For the Board

Between 2024 and 2026, groups calling themselves pro-Russia hacktivists, which the governments of Denmark and Poland say act for the Russian state, broke into the control systems of small water utilities in the United States and Europe. They did not pick their targets; they took whatever they could reach from the internet, usually through a remote screen with a weak or factory password. In Texas a tank overflowed. In Denmark about 450 homes lost water when the pumps were switched off, and pipes burst the next day when they were switched to full speed. No one was hurt, and each utility recovered by hand or with its supplier’s help. A utility of any size whose control screens can be reached from the internet, or whose passwords are weak, is in the population these groups scan; size and location do not protect it. Most of the protection costs little: taking the screens off the internet, changing passwords, and practicing manual operation. The protection that matters most, equipment in the field that stops a pump or a tank from being driven past safe limits whatever the screen says, may already be in place and needs only to be checked. The self-check in this annex tells management where the utility stands.
