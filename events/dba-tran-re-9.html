The Fracture in the Hernando de Soto Bridge, May 2021

Event date: 11 May 2021 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 11 May 2021, inspectors working on the Hernando de Soto Bridge, which carries Interstate 40 over the Mississippi River at Memphis, saw a fracture in one of its main steel tie girders and called 911. The highway was closed for eighty-three days and the river beneath it for about three. The crack had grown from welding defects made when the bridge was fabricated and, on the state's account, was visible in earlier images, though the bridge had been inspected every year. Testing found twelve more cracked welds on the bridge, and federal regulators ordered such members tested nationwide.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: a fabrication defect, on the forensic analyst's account, found before the span fell. It is an accidental structural fracture, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, An inspection is credited for what it is shown to have reached. The span was inspected every year and the crack was missed for years.
  • F-2, The postulated failure names the fabrication defect, and its population. The crack grew from welding defects, so finding one should open a review of every member that shares them.
  • F-3, What a span already records is read for its critical members. A drone video and sensor data held the evidence and were not read for it.
  • F-4, A critical finding reaches the closure by a named path. The finding reached the closure by a 911 call from inspectors working outside their scope.
  • F-5, The channel beneath a span in doubt reopens on a stated basis. The river reopened on modeling before the span was stabilized, and the basis for reopening should be written down.
  • F-6, A repair time includes the search for the defect elsewhere on the span. The repair ran to the eighty-third day because more cracked welds were found and fixed.
  • F-7, The alternative is counted with the hazard it shares. The one other nearby crossing shares the region's earthquake hazard.
  • F-8, The nonredundant tension member is a target, and its record a reading. The design basis should protect such members and the records on which decisions to close or reopen rest.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owners and inspectors of bridges with nonredundant steel tension members, those whose failure may bring down part or all of a span, and for the consultants who work on such bridges whatever their scope. It is also for any owner, in any sector, of a structure whose integrity rests on single members. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Inspect for What You Reach, and Find the Defect’s Population

The Federal Highway Administration required the States in December 2021 to identify fracture critical members of T-1 steel fabricated without a fracture control plan, to test their butt welds in tension by nondestructive methods by 31 March 2024, and to treat rejectable indications as critical findings. The National Bridge Inspection Standards of 2022 set the interval for a nonredundant steel tension member at not more than 24 months and define its inspection as hands-on, within arm’s length of the member. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1 and F-2: credit an inspection only for the faces and welds its record shows were reached by hand; rotate inspectors in practice and have the work reviewed independently; and record every federal advisory on a kind of member against each of your structures that has one until it is answered.

A.3.2 Read What You Already Hold, and Name the Path to Closure

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-4. Read the images, sensor data, and records your structures already hold for their critical members, whatever they were taken for, and keep any instrumentation maintained and reviewed for structural change; and let anyone you admit to a critical structure report a critical finding directly to the person who can close it, and to the Captain of the Port where it crosses navigable water, by a named path that does not wait on review.

A.3.3 Plan the Reopening and the Repair

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-5, F-6, and F-7. Write in advance the basis on which you would ask that the channel beneath a span in doubt be reopened; state the repair time as stabilization, repair of the member, and the search for the same defect elsewhere on the span; and credit an alternative crossing only for the events that would not reach it too.

A.4 For the Board

On 11 May 2021, inspectors working on the Hernando de Soto Bridge, which carries Interstate 40 over the Mississippi River at Memphis, saw a fracture in one of its main steel tie girders, a member whose failure could bring down part of the bridge. They were inspecting other parts of the bridge and called 911. The highway was closed for eighty-three days and the river beneath for about three, with more than a thousand barges waiting. The crack had grown from welding defects made when the bridge was fabricated, and the state said it was visible in a 2019 drone video and in a 2016 photograph. The bridge had been inspected every year, but the state said the face where the crack lay had not been inspected by hand. Testing then found twelve more cracked welds on the same bridge, and federal regulators ordered such members tested nationwide. No one was hurt, and no one suggests the fracture was intended. The lessons are that an inspection is worth what it actually reached, not the fact that it was done, and that the evidence already in our own files must be read. Any owner whose structures rest on single members has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
