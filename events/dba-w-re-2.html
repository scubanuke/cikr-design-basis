The Oldsmar Water Treatment Incident, February 2021

Event date: 5 February 2021 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 5 February 2021 an operator at the Oldsmar, Florida, water treatment plant saw a workstation's cursor move without local input as the sodium hydroxide setpoint was raised from about 100 to about 11,100 parts per million, and set it back at once. The workstation was reachable through shared remote-support software, with shared credentials, on an unsupported operating system. Its description as an external cyberattack was later disputed by an official in office at the time and was not confirmed by the federal investigation.

Tier

No adversary established. Not military action: intentionality is indeterminate, persistence and denial are absent, and precision is low.

Findings

  • F-1, The design basis reading is independent of the contested attribution. The access path is the hazard whoever used it.
  • F-2, Oldsmar does not satisfy the military-action characteristics; the extension rests on a constructed analog. The framework's military-action reading of the event is a construction, not the event.
  • F-3, The detection-independence lesson was drawn from a case where detection succeeded and indication was true. The operator saw a true display.
  • F-4, The access vector, shared remote-support tooling and an unsupported host, is only implicitly addressed. The common case in small systems is not named.
  • F-5, The sole effective barrier was human observation under favorable physics. Harm was averted because someone happened to be watching.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for drinking water utilities, especially small systems, whose operators reach treatment controls through remote-access software, and for the staff who manage their credentials and workstations. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Govern Remote Access, Credentials, and the Host

The advisory of February 2021, AA21-042A, directed attention to remote-access hygiene, credential practice, and operating-system currency in the sector. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-4: name the specific access surface of this event, shared remote-support tooling, shared credentials, and an unsupported host, in the utility’s own controls, since it is the common case in small systems.

A.3.2 Put an Engineered Limit Behind the Operator

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1, F-3, and F-5. Treat the path from a control action to a physical consequence as the hazard, whoever’s hand is on the interface; and give a consequence-bearing setpoint an engineered limit or independent check, so that harm is not averted only because someone happened to be watching.

A.3.3 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the path in this event was remote-support software, which commonly connects out through its vendor’s relay service, so a search of the plant’s own addresses may not show it. List every remote-support tool installed on a process workstation, and remove any that is not needed. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element A-1 and F-4.

A.4 For the Board

On the afternoon of 5 February 2021, an operator at the water treatment plant in Oldsmar, Florida, serving about fifteen thousand people, saw the cursor on a supervisory workstation move without local input, and the sodium hydroxide setpoint changed from about 100 to about 11,100 parts per million. The operator set it back at once; officials said the change would have taken a day or more to reach customers and that other monitoring would have caught it. The workstation was reachable through remote-support software, with credentials shared among staff, on an operating system past its vendor’s support. At the time it was described as a cyberattack, and a federal advisory treated it as one; later, an official in office at the time disputed that account, and the federal investigation did not confirm it. This instrument does not resolve the dispute, and its findings do not depend on it. The lessons are that the path from a control to a physical consequence is the hazard whoever is at the interface, that shared credentials erase the difference between an intruder and an operator, and that harm averted by someone happening to watch is not protection. Any utility reaching its controls remotely has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this utility does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
