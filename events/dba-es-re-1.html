The Attack on the Metcalf Transmission Substation, near San Jose, California, 16 April 2013

Event date: 16 April 2013 (record open as to the investigation) · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Lifelines collection

Impacted sector: Communications, Emergency Services

What happened

In the early hours of 16 April 2013 AT&T's and Level 3's cables were cut in two vaults near the Pacific Gas and Electric Company's 500 kV Metcalf substation, and from about 1:37 a.m. gunfire from outside the perimeter struck seventeen transformers for about nineteen minutes, draining their cooling oil; police arrived at 1:51 with the attackers gone. Telephone service in nearby towns was lost for about a day and 911 calls in the Gilroy area for a couple of hours; the utility's own SCADA held. Power was rerouted, there were no outages, and the station was back in service after 27 days. The attack led to the CIP-014 physical-security standard.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. Deliberate adversarial, below the tier: intentionality and precision established, persistence and denial not established.

Findings

  • F-1, The class's account is corrected. Metcalf is a 500 kV station, the nine-substation analysis is an unreleased staff study reported in the press, the attack began with a cut to the area's communications, and the station was back in 27 days.
  • F-2, A transformer's cooling is repaired in weeks. Damage to cooling made the transformers inoperative and was repaired in 27 days; the class's two-year figure belongs to destroyed transformers, not to this event.
  • F-3, Persistence is read from the adversary. The attackers stayed about nineteen minutes, and the record shows no return; a transformer's lead time is how long a consequence lasts, not a characteristic of the attacker.
  • F-4, An attack can begin with the area's communications. Two carriers' cables were cut in vaults within sight of each other; the alarms came through on a backup path the attacker missed, and the carriers' outages were never joined to the attack across sectors.
  • F-5, Protection is measured in minutes. Police arrived fourteen minutes after the fence alarm, which came among alarms the wind had been setting off; a perimeter alarm that wind can trip cannot tell anyone that a person is at the fence.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for owners and operators of transmission substations, the transmission operators and reliability coordinators who operate the system around them, and the law enforcement agencies and telecommunications carriers on whom a station’s protection depends. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to an owner. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an owner answers about its own stations (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an owner can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

After the attack NERC’s Electricity Sector Information Sharing and Analysis Center issued an alert to the industry on steps to mitigate and protect against such attacks, and the Department of Energy and the Department of Homeland Security, with the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Commission, and NERC, briefed utilities, states, and law enforcement on the attack in thirteen cities. The Commission’s order of 7 March 2014 and its Order No. 802 of 20 November 2014 put CIP-014 in place: owners and operators of the most critical facilities identify them, assess the threats and vulnerabilities to them, and put security plans in place that are independently reviewed, and the standard has since been revised. The Spare Transformer Equipment Program, launched by the Edison Electric Institute in 2006, has its participants maintain spare transformers to share after a triggering event (Congressional Research Service). The utility’s own program after the attack, of perimeter barriers, equipment shielding, cameras, and vegetation clearing, is its own account. Each is its author’s.

A.3.2 Keep the Alarm Path Off the Local Lines, Measure the Response, and Plan the Repair

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2, F-4, and F-5. Give a station’s alarms at least two physically separate routes to its security monitoring and law enforcement, counting routes and not carriers, keep a backup path that shares no route with them, and test them with the local routes out, on the assumption that an attacker will look for every path; arrange with the carriers, or through the sectors’ information sharing centers, to learn of an outage near a station while it is happening; make perimeter detection tell weather from a person, so that nuisance alarms are reduced at their source rather than silenced; measure the station’s protection by how quickly an attack is detected and answered, and test that too; and plan the repair of damage to transformers’ cooling in weeks, with the spares and crews it needs, as a case separate from the replacement of a destroyed transformer.

A.4 For the Board

In the early hours of 16 April 2013, someone cut two carriers’ telecommunications cables in vaults near the Metcalf substation, a 500 kilovolt station of the Pacific Gas and Electric Company south of San Jose. Shortly afterwards, gunfire from outside the fence struck seventeen transformers for about nineteen minutes, draining their cooling oil, and the attackers were gone before police arrived fourteen minutes after the fence alarm. Telephone and internet service in nearby towns was lost for about a day, and 911 calls in the Gilroy area failed for a couple of hours. Grid operators rerouted power and asked the public to conserve, no customer lost power, and the station was back in service after 27 days. The station’s alarms reached the control center over a backup path the attackers did not account for, but they arrived among alarms the wind had been setting off all night, and the carriers’ outages were never joined to the attack outside the two sectors that saw them. No arrest has been reported. The attack led federal regulators to require a physical-security standard for the most critical stations. The lessons are that an attack on a station can begin by cutting the communications of its area, and two carriers cut at one place are one path, not two, that a station’s alarms need a path an attacker cannot see and must be able to tell a person from the wind, that protection is measured by how fast an attack is detected and answered, and that a month’s repair is a different problem from losing a transformer for two years. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own stations, and most of the answers cost little to find.
