The Congestion of the San Pedro Bay Ports, 2020 to 2022

Event date: October 2020 to November 2022 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

From October 2020, a surge in imports outran what the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach and the trucks, chassis, rail, and warehouses behind them could clear, and container ships queued offshore for twenty-five months. The ports moved record volumes in 2021 while ships waited two weeks or more, and the queue peaked at 109 vessels on 9 January 2022. In the crowded anchorage, a storm dragged two ships' anchors over an oil pipeline that later ruptured; the backlog cleared as demand eased and cargo moved to other coasts.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act, and nothing was broken: a surge of demand beyond the clearing capacity of the gateway for about 40 percent of the country's containers, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, Flow delayed at a node that never stopped. The ports set records while ships waited, so the design basis should count delay as well as lost flow.
  • F-2, The clearing capacity was set beyond the gate. What bound the ports was how fast trucks, chassis, rail, and warehouses took containers away.
  • F-3, The queue held beyond the fence was a hazard there. The full anchorage put ships over a pipeline, and the queue length that would fill the anchorages should be stated.
  • F-4, The queue was managed by an arrangement made nine months after the anchorages filled. The order and place of a queue should be agreed before it forms.
  • F-5, Diversion was counted once, because the surge struck every path. The other ports queued too, so diversion capacity cannot be counted twice.
  • F-6, The backlog cleared in months, on the timetable of demand. The backlog fell over about ten and a half months from its peak, because demand fell, not because capacity rose.
  • F-7, The levers on a queue were unstated and unmeasured. Extended hours, fees, and queue rules did little, and none had been measured in advance.
  • F-8, A node at its limit, and a full anchorage, are what the adversary would choose. The design basis should be tested with the port and its alternatives already at surged flow.
  • F-9, The gap the class fills, confirmed. No rule sets how long a ship may wait off a port or how full its anchorage may be.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for port authorities, terminal operators, and the operators of other transportation nodes whose flow can, for weeks or months, exceed what they and the chains behind them can clear. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose site can keep working at full pace while the work waiting for it grows. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Measure Delay, and Know What Sets Your Clearing Rate

This step is this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2. Count waiting, at anchor and on the terminal, beyond your normal wait, beside your throughput; state your clearing rate as the least of your berth rate, your yard capacity, and the rate at which the chains beyond your gate take flow away, with what you count on of trucks, chassis, rail, and storage; and watch dwell time against its normal value as the sign that the limit is near.

A.3.2 Plan the Queue and Where It Waits

The National Transportation Safety Board found the probable cause of the anchor strike on the San Pedro Bay Pipeline to be the proximity of established anchorage positions to the pipeline, recommended that the Coast Guard restructure the anchorages, and recommended an alarm in the vessel traffic service’s monitoring system for such an encroachment. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3 and F-4: state the length of queue that would fill the places your traffic waits at the spacing their safe use requires, and fix in advance, with the vessel traffic service and the parties at the port, the order in which waiting vessels are worked, where they wait, and who decides.

A.3.3 Count Diversion Honestly and Measure Your Levers

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-5, F-6, and F-7. Credit diversion only for what the other ports can take at their own surged flow, since a surge in demand reaches them all; state the measures you would take when flow exceeds your clearing rate, the parties beyond the gate each depends on, and their expected effect; and credit none whose effect has not been measured.

A.4 For the Board

From October 2020 to November 2022, container ships queued off the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach, the point of entry for about 40 percent of the containers coming into the United States on the White House’s figure. Demand surged beyond what the ports and the trucks, chassis, rail, and warehouses behind them could clear. The ports moved record volumes in 2021, yet ships waited two weeks or more, and at the peak, on 9 January 2022, 109 vessels were in the queue. In the crowded anchorage a storm dragged two ships’ anchors across an oil pipeline, which ruptured months later. Extended hours, a threatened fee, and a new queuing system did little; the backlog cleared when demand fell and cargo moved to other coasts, whose ports then queued in turn. Nothing broke, and no one attacked. The lesson is that a site can be working at a record pace and still be failing, if the measure is volume and not delay, and that the limit that binds may lie beyond the gate. Any operation whose flow can outrun what it and its partners can clear has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
