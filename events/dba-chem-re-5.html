The Fire and Explosion at West Fertilizer, April 2013

Event date: 17 April 2013 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

On the evening of 17 April 2013, a fire at the West Fertilizer Company in West, Texas, reached about forty to sixty tons of ammonium nitrate stored in wooden bins in a wood-framed building, and about twenty minutes after the fire was reported the pile detonated. Fifteen people died, twelve of them responders, more than 260 were injured, and schools, a nursing home, and apartments nearby were destroyed or damaged. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives determined that the fire was intentionally set, and its investigation is open.

Tier

No adversary established. Not military action: whatever lit the fire, the explosion and its reach followed, on the Chemical Safety Board's analysis, from how and where the material was stored and how the response unfolded.

Findings

  • F-1, What makes a store detonable in a burning building. Combustible construction, nearby combustibles, and no detection or sprinklers likely contributed to the detonation.
  • F-2, The distance is held by two parties. Schools, a nursing home, and apartments were built near the facility over the years.
  • F-3, In advance means a plan, for everyone who comes. There was no plan, incident command, or public alert, and twelve of the dead were responders.
  • F-4, The ammonia beside the nitrate. Ammonia vessels nearby were damaged and leaked, a near-miss.
  • F-5, The source term, the railcar, and what stood near. A nearby railcar of the same material overturned without detonating.
  • F-6, A fire whatever its cause. The fire's cause is contested, and the consequence did not depend on it.
  • F-7, In the deliberate analog, the response is part of the consequence. A set fire draws responders and onlookers toward the store before it detonates.
  • F-8, Withdrawn. Its completion of the facility class's regulatory statement went to the class as an erratum.
  • F-9, Who holds the design basis for a small retailer. No outside party checked the facility's storage against a standard.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its qualification of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.
  • F-11, Material awaiting disposal belongs to the class. Carried from the withdrawn Beirut instrument: a cargo held for years by no facility fell under no one's design basis.
  • F-12, The disposition decision had no holder. Carried from the withdrawn Beirut instrument: many authorities held part of the decision and none the whole.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for retailers, blenders, and distributors that store ammonium nitrate or other materials that can detonate, the fire departments that would answer a fire there, and the local governments that decide what may be built nearby. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Store It So a Fire Cannot Detonate It

After the event, Texas adopted rules in 2014 and a statute in 2015, and the 2016 edition of NFPA 400 prohibited combustible construction for new ammonium nitrate storage. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-4, and F-5: store ammonium nitrate in noncombustible construction, apart from other combustibles, with detection or sprinklers; and site other hazards on the property so that a blast cannot reach them.

A.3.2 Plan the Response, Including the Withdrawal

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3, F-6, and F-7. Prepare a plan with every department that would come, covering incident command, water supply, a public alert, and when to stand back and withdraw from a fire that may detonate, whatever caused the fire; and plan for onlookers drawn to the scene.

A.3.3 Hold the Distance, and Name Who Checks

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2 and F-9. Treat the distance between the store and schools, homes, and care facilities as held by the business and the local government together; and make sure some party outside the business, a producer’s certification as one producer adopted after the event, an insurer, or a regulator, checks the store against a standard.

A.4 For the Board

On the evening of 17 April 2013, a fire at the West Fertilizer Company in West, Texas, reached about forty to sixty tons of ammonium nitrate stored in wooden bins in a wood-framed building, and about twenty minutes after the fire was reported the pile detonated. Fifteen people died, twelve of them responders, and more than 260 were injured. Two schools, a nursing home, and apartments built near the facility over the years were destroyed or damaged. The building had no detection or sprinklers, the nearest hydrant was too far, and there was no plan for a fire there. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives determined in 2016 that the fire was intentionally set, and its investigation remains open; the Chemical Safety Board, whose report is the record, found that storage and siting set the consequence, whatever lit the fire. The lessons are that such material must be stored so that a fire cannot detonate it, that responders must have a plan that includes withdrawal, and that the distance to schools and homes must be held. Any business that stores material that can explode has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this business does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
