The Compromise of Eight eMudhra Intermediate Certificate Authorities, 2025 and 2026

Event date: April 2025 to October 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.9 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

In April 2025 eMudhra created eight intermediate signing keys whose private keys, through a script error on its account, anyone could recover from the public certificates, allowing forgery of website and Windows code-signing identities. An outside researcher reported it on 17 October 2026; browsers blocked the intermediates within hours, and the root was distrusted. No misuse is recorded, but unattended clients without transparency or revocation checking stay exposed until their trust stores refresh.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: a defect in a key ceremony, with no misuse recorded.

Findings

  • F-1, Two paths on one anchor are not independent. Paths through two roots rested on the same compromised keys.
  • F-2, Exposure is set by the client's architecture, and the classes do not state it. Browsers were protected in hours; unattended clients were not.
  • F-3, The browser's containment does not reach the infrastructure client. Infrastructure clients seldom check revocation or transparency.
  • F-4, The blast radius of a trust anchor is bounded by its consumers' containment. One defect reached clients in every sector.
  • F-5, The origin evidence rests on an authority the vendor does not hold. Code signed under the recoverable keys would be accepted as a vendor's.
  • F-6, Trust-store refresh is the residual-risk variable. Offline and embedded clients carry the exposure longest.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for operators of systems that trust certificates, especially unattended and embedded clients in industrial, utility, and other infrastructure that do not check certificate transparency or revocation, and for the certificate authorities whose ceremonies create the keys. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Contain at the Client, and Count Shared Anchors as One

On the root programs’ records, Chrome blocked the six TLS intermediates within hours of the report on 17 October 2026, Mozilla blocked all eight the same day, the issuer revoked them the next morning, Apple added protections on 19 October, and Microsoft blocked the certificates on 20 October through its certificate trust list updates. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, F-3, and F-4: count two certificate paths that rest on one anchor as one; and give infrastructure clients the containment browsers have, revocation and transparency checking, or state that they lack it.

A.3.2 Refresh Trust Stores, and Know Who Holds the Origin

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-5 and F-6. Treat the time to refresh each client’s trust store as the residual-risk variable, and shorten it most for offline, air-gapped, and embedded clients; and know which authority your code-signing evidence rests on, since a vendor does not hold the root its signatures chain to.

A.3.3 List What Your Unattended Clients Trust

The exposure in this event was not a service anyone could scan for. It was the set of certificates an unattended device trusts, which no search of the internet can see, so the inventory here is of trust stores, not addresses. List each unattended, offline, or embedded client, the trust store it uses, how and when that store is refreshed, and the roots and intermediates in it. Mark each anchor the client does not need, and ask the device’s vendor how it can be removed and how fast a revocation reaches the device. The inventory is the form the standing exposure item takes in this annex, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here it rests on element E-3 and F-5.

A.4 For the Board

In April 2025 a certificate authority, eMudhra, created eight intermediate signing keys whose private keys, on the issuer’s account through a script error, were small numbers anyone could recover from the public certificates. Six could vouch for any website’s identity and two for any Windows software publisher’s, through roots widely trusted. Invalid signatures in 2025 were attributed to another cause and the matter closed. An outside researcher found the weakness on 17 October 2026 and reported it; browsers blocked the intermediates within hours, the issuer revoked them the next day, operating-system vendors followed within days, and the root was distrusted, leaving about 30,000 certificates to be replaced. No misuse is recorded, and browsers that check public transparency logs can show none occurred against them. But unattended clients that do not check transparency or revocation, common in infrastructure, stay exposed until their trust stores are refreshed, and there is no public record either way for them. No one attacked anything. The lessons are that two certificate paths to one anchor are one path, that the containment browsers have does not reach most infrastructure clients, and that the time to refresh a trust store is the time a client stays exposed. Any operator of systems that trust certificates has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
