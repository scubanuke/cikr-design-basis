The Shortage of Transformers in the United States, 2021 Onward

Event date: 2021 onward · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

Since 2021 average transformer lead times in the United States have risen from about 50 weeks to about 120, and large units to as much as four years, as new loads, an aging fleet, and constrained manufacturing outran supply. Imports supply about 80 percent of power transformers. The National Infrastructure Advisory Council recommended in 2024 support for domestic capacity, a virtual reserve, and standardized designs.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: demand outran supply over a condition of years.

Findings

  • F-1, The national queue is measured by someone with a duty to watch it. Every lead-time figure read came from analysts and an advisory council, not from a body charged to watch.
  • F-2, The source count is stated with its trade exposure. Imports supply about 80 percent of power transformers, and tariffs raised their cost.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for electric utilities, developers, and large industrial customers that buy power and distribution transformers, for the manufacturers that make them, and for the agencies that watch the national supply. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Watch the Queue, Standardize, and Share a Reserve

The National Infrastructure Advisory Council recommended in June 2024 federal support for domestic capacity, a strategic virtual reserve of transformers with the government as buyer of last resort, and standardization of designs. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1: track the lead time of each type you depend on, forecast your own demand, and take part in a shared reserve or standard design where you can.

A.3.2 State Your Source Count With Its Trade Exposure

This step is this instrument’s, from F-2. Know how many qualified sources you have for each type and how many lie abroad, and what tariffs or trade decisions would do to them.

A.4 For the Board

Since 2021 the United States has been short of transformers. Average lead times rose from about 50 weeks in 2021 to about 120 weeks in 2024, and large units ran up to four years. The National Infrastructure Advisory Council reported in June 2024 that the causes include supply-chain disruption, an aging fleet whose average large power transformer is about 38 years old, new loads such as data centers, and an industry that holds capacity back through its cycles, with little standardization of designs. Imports supply about 80 percent of power transformers, and an analyst projected a 30 percent shortfall for 2025, with constraints into the 2030s. The Council recommended federal support for domestic manufacturing, a virtual reserve with the government as buyer of last resort, and standardized designs. No one attacked anything; demand simply outran supply. The lessons are that someone must watch the national queue, that the dependence on imports must be stated, and that a utility must plan for waits of years. Any utility or large customer that needs transformers has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this organization does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
