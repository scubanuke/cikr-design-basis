The Crowd Crush at the Astroworld Festival, November 2021

Event date: 5 November 2021 · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

At the Astroworld festival in Houston on 5 November 2021, about fifty thousand people attended a site where no occupancy load had been issued, and the crowd pressed toward the stage. Warnings began at 9:00 p.m., a mass-casualty incident was declared at 9:38 p.m., and the show ended about 10:10 p.m. Ten people died of compression asphyxia; a grand jury declined to indict anyone.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: a crowd crush with no adversary.

Findings

  • F-1, The stop has triggers, and the response's declaration is one. The show went on for half an hour after a mass-casualty declaration.
  • F-2, The plan binds every authority that permits or answers. The county permitted the event and the city answered it.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for promoters and producers of large festivals and concerts, the venues and sites that host them, and the county and city authorities that permit them and answer their emergencies. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Permit Through One Plan That Binds Every Authority

The Texas Task Force on Concert Safety found the permitting process inconsistent across the state, found that no occupancy load had been issued, and recommended a universal permitting template. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-2: make the event plan bind every authority that permits or answers it, county and city alike.

A.3.2 Write the Stop Triggers in Advance, and Give Someone the Authority

The task force recommended a unified command group on site that includes the local 911 agencies, with a production representative holding authority to stop the show; triggers for pausing or stopping the show written into the permit and agreed in advance; and event-specific training, walkthroughs, and tabletop exercises. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-1: count the response’s own declaration of a mass-casualty incident as a trigger that stops the show.

A.4 For the Board

On 5 November 2021, at the Astroworld festival in a parking lot at NRG Park in Houston, about fifty thousand people attended, and people without tickets had breached the perimeter hours before the headlining set. As the crowd pressed toward the stage, warnings reached the event’s staff from 9:00 p.m., a mass-casualty incident was declared at 9:38 p.m., and the show did not end until about 10:10 p.m. Ten people, aged nine to twenty-seven, died of compression asphyxia, and hundreds were injured. No occupancy load had been issued; the event was permitted by the county and answered by the city. A grand jury declined to indict anyone, and the state’s task force recommended a single permitting template, a unified command on site with authority to stop the show, and stop triggers agreed in advance. No one intended harm. The lessons are that an event must have one plan binding every authority, that the triggers to stop it must be written before the gates open, and that someone on site must hold the authority to act on them. Any large event has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this event does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
