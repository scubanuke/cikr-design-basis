The Intrusion Into the Bowman Avenue Dam, 2013

Event date: 28 August to 18 September 2013 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

In August and September 2013, on the United States Attorney's account, an intruder repeatedly reached the control system of the Bowman Avenue Dam, a small flood-control structure in Rye Brook, New York, and read its water levels and the status of its sluice gate. The gate could not be moved only because it had been disconnected for maintenance. The county's emergency managers learned of the intrusion from the press more than two years later.

Tier

Military action, tier not established. Not military action: it occurred in peacetime, and the United States Government, which links it to a state, proceeded by criminal indictment, whose charges are untried.

Findings

  • F-1, Read access to a gate's controls is a compromise of the gate. The intruder read the gate's status through the system that commands it.
  • F-2, An intrusion into a gate's controls is reported to those who warn the valley. Emergency managers learned of it from the press.
  • F-3, No federal security requirement reached this dam. The dam is a municipal structure outside federal jurisdiction.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for owners of small dams and other water-control works whose gates are operated by computer, the municipal and county agencies that own and oversee them, and the emergency managers who warn the valleys below. It is also for any owner of a control system that can be reached from a network. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know Every Path, and Treat Reading as Compromise

A Department of Homeland Security bulletin of 2014, as the instrument cites it, described a control system reached by cellular modem as directly connected to the internet. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1: record and verify every remote path to a gate’s controls; treat read access to them as a compromise of the gate; and make remote operation a governed act, not one prevented by chance.

A.3.2 Tell Those Who Warn, and Have a Plan

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2 and F-3. Report an intrusion into a gate’s controls to the emergency managers who warn the valley; and keep an emergency action plan for the dam, whether or not a federal requirement reaches it.

A.3.3 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the intrusion on this record came through a cellular modem, which takes its address from the carrier, not from the dam’s owner. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element I-1 and F-1.

A.4 For the Board

Between 28 August and 18 September 2013, on the United States Attorney’s account, an intruder repeatedly reached the control system of the Bowman Avenue Dam, a small flood-control structure in Rye Brook, New York, and read its water levels, temperature, and the status of its sluice gate. Remote operation of the gate would have been possible had it not been disconnected for maintenance. The city was told soon after; the county’s emergency managers learned of it from the press more than two years later. In 2016 the Justice Department indicted the person charged with the intrusion as part of a group it said acted on behalf of the government of Iran; that is the United States Government’s statement, and the charges are untried. The dam was reported in 2022 to have no emergency plan. The lessons are that every path into a gate’s controls must be known, that reading the controls is a compromise of the gate, and that the people who warn the valley must be told. Any owner of a computer-operated gate has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this dam does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
