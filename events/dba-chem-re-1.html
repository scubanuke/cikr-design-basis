The TRITON Attack on a Petrochemical Safety System, 2017

Event date: 2017 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

In 2017, on the accounts of the United States government and the investigators, an intruder spent almost a year in the networks of a petrochemical plant in the Middle East before reaching the engineering workstation of its Triconex safety controllers and installing malware designed to make the safety system unsafe while it appeared normal. An error in the malware tripped the plant to a safe shutdown twice, in June and August 2017; the first trip was taken for a mechanical glitch, and the second led to discovery. Nothing was released and no one was hurt.

Tier

Military action, tier not established. Not military action, though it is the nearest the record offers: the governments that attribute it describe a deliberate and persistent capability for physical harm, but it came in peacetime, alone, and ended in a safe shutdown.

Findings

  • F-1, The safety system was not independent, and the class requires it to be. The safety system could be reached from the corporate and operations networks.
  • F-2, A governed act left standing is not governed. The controller's key switch was left in PROGRAM.
  • F-3, The firmware beneath the logic. The malware used a firmware flaw to run in the controller's memory regardless of the key switch.
  • F-4, The design failed safe because the attacker erred. The shutdown came from a mistake in the malware, not from the design defeating it.
  • F-5, The intrusion passed its first trip. The plant restarted after the first trip with the intruder still inside.
  • F-6, The determinism exemption rests on the installed configuration. The function running was no longer the function tested as installed.
  • F-7, The adversary behaved as the class expects. The actor stayed for months and chose the safety layer over the control system.
  • F-8, Withdrawn. Its qualification of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for owners and operators of chemical, petrochemical, and refining plants whose processes are protected by safety instrumented systems, and for the engineers who program and maintain those systems. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose last protective layer is a programmable controller. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Isolate the Safety System, and Govern the Key Switch

The advisories published in December 2017, by the vendor, the government, and the investigators, converge: keep the key switch out of PROGRAM except during scheduled programming, alarm its state at operator stations, isolate the safety network, and connect the engineering workstation to no other network. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-1 and F-2: treat each change of key-switch position as a deliberate, authenticated, and recorded act, never left standing.

A.3.2 Correct the Firmware, and Check What Is Running

The government advisory ICSA-18-107-02 identifies the controller vulnerabilities the malware used, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation reports that the vendor corrected them in firmware released in June 2018 and that older controllers remain in use and vulnerable. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3 and F-6: bring safety controllers to corrected firmware, and verify periodically that the firmware and logic running are those tested as installed.

A.3.3 Treat an Unexplained Trip as a Possible Intrusion

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4, F-5, and F-7. Investigate an unexplained safety trip for intrusion before restarting; do not count on an attacker’s error to make the design fail safe; and look for an actor that stays for months, works off-hours, and returns to learn how the plant responds.

A.4 For the Board

In 2017, on the accounts of the United States government and the investigators, an intruder spent almost a year in the networks of a petrochemical plant in the Middle East before reaching the engineering workstation of its Triconex safety controllers, the last automated layer that shuts the process down when it becomes dangerous. The malware was designed to make the safety system unsafe while it appeared normal. It failed: an error in it tripped the plant to a safe shutdown twice, and the first trip was taken for a mechanical glitch, so the plant restarted with the intruder inside. The United States and the United Kingdom attribute the attack to a Russian government research institute; that is their attribution. Nothing was released and no one was hurt, but the governments state the capability was for an explosion, a toxic release, and loss of life. The lessons are that the safety system must be isolated from every other network, that its programming switch must never be left open, and that an unexplained trip may be an intrusion. Any plant protected by a programmable safety system has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this plant does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
