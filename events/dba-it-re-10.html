The Forging of Identity Tokens With a Stolen Microsoft Signing Key, 2023

Event date: May and June 2023, read from the key's creation in 2016 (record open in part) · Read from instrument version 0.3 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

Using tokens signed with a 2016 Microsoft consumer signing key that a flaw let work for enterprise accounts, an actor the Cyber Safety Review Board and Microsoft assess to be affiliated with the People's Republic of China reached the Exchange Online mailboxes of 22 organizations and more than 500 individuals. The Department of State found it through records not all customers held. Microsoft did not know how or when the key was obtained.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Met, recorded as military action at the tier on the Board's record: intentionality, persistence, and precision established, and denial established as denial of function on the signing key, on the author's ruling of 20 November 2026.

Findings

  • F-1, A Valid Signature on a Token No One Issued. A forged token is evident whatever the key's history.
  • F-2, The Key's Function Was Denied Though Nothing Was Stopped. Denial is established on the key, not on the mail.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for organizations that rely on a cloud identity provider for their people’s and services’ sign-in, the providers themselves, and those who oversee them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to an organization. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an organization answers about its own identity services (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an organization can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one organization. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the organization; it is that organization’s finding, and the place to start. Because the event’s access ran through a service reachable from the internet, the standing exposure item is asked first.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

The Board’s review set out its findings and its recommendations to Microsoft and to cloud providers, and federal guidance on the logging available to tenants of cloud services followed. The class’s requirements (§4.5, §5.5) state protected, rotated, and scoped keys, relying services that check scope, tenant access to the records, and the ability to revoke trust and move authentication in a stated time. Each is its author’s.

A.3.2 Ask How a Forged Token Would Be Told From a Real One

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1. Ask your identity provider how a token it did not issue would be told from one it did, at the moment it is presented, and do not rest your critical services on a signature alone if the answer is that it cannot.

A.4 For the Board

In the summer of 2023, an espionage group that the United States government’s Cyber Safety Review Board and Microsoft assess to be affiliated with China read the email of 22 organizations and more than 500 people, including senior United States officials. It did so by using a Microsoft signing key, the digital stamp that tells online services a sign-in is genuine, created in 2016 for consumer accounts. A flaw let that key work for business and government accounts too. The intrusion was found by the State Department, which had paid for records most customers did not have, and Microsoft could not say how or when the key had been taken. The lessons are that a single key at a large provider can open doors across every sector, and that we should ask our providers how a forged sign-in would be detected. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own identity services, and most of the answers cost little to find.
