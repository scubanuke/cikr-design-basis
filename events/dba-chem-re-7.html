The LockerGoga Ransomware Attacks, March 2019

Event date: December 2018 to March 2019 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

In March 2019, LockerGoga ransomware struck two related chemical companies in the United States, Hexion and Momentive, and the Norwegian aluminum producer Norsk Hydro within days of each other. At Hydro the attackers had entered months earlier and pushed the ransomware from the company's own domain controllers to every joined machine. Business systems were lost at all three companies; the chemical plants, on separate networks, ran safely, and Hydro worked by hand, restored from backups, and paid no ransom.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate criminal attack, not military action: no government has attributed it to a state, and no safety incident at any site was reported.

Findings

  • F-1, Fleet reach runs through the domain. The ransomware was pushed from the company's own domain controllers to every joined machine.
  • F-2, The plants that stopped were waiting on production systems. Some plants stopped only because they lost connection to production systems.
  • F-3, Manual fallback and the response outward took people, stock, channels, and notice. Manual operation took stock, paper, retirees, and new channels to customers.
  • F-4, A compromise found at the center starts the same response. Hydro isolated every plant and verified containment before recovering.
  • F-5, Withdrawn. Its substance is carried by F-3.
  • F-6, Withdrawn. Its substance is carried by F-3.
  • F-7, Restoration rested on backups beyond the attack. Hydro rebuilt from backups the attack had not reached.
  • F-8, Two companies sharing services share reach. The two chemical companies shared technology services.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its qualification of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for chemical and process companies that run many sites from a shared enterprise network, and for the technology teams that administer their domains. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose plants could be reached by a change pushed from the center. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Separate the Plants From the Domain

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads; Microsoft’s and the companies’ accounts are its record. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1: learn which plant machines are joined to or reachable from the enterprise domain, separate those that bear on the process, and watch for an intruder who holds administrative credentials for months.

A.3.2 Let the Plants Run Alone, and Prepare the Manual Fallback

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2 and F-3. Make sure plants can run safely without the enterprise’s production systems; and plan the manual fallback in people, stock, paper, and channels outward to customers and authorities, for weeks.

A.3.3 Isolate, Verify, and Restore From Backups Beyond Reach

Norsk Hydro isolated all its plants, disconnected every server and PC, verified containment before recovery, rebuilt from backups, and paid no ransom. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-4, F-7, and F-8: treat a compromise found at the center as starting the same isolation; keep backups beyond the attacker’s reach; and know what a shared services agreement with another company lets an attacker reach.

A.4 For the Board

In March 2019, LockerGoga ransomware struck two related chemical companies in the United States, Hexion and Momentive, and the Norwegian aluminum producer Norsk Hydro within days of each other. At Hydro, on Microsoft’s account, the attackers had entered in December 2018 through an infected attachment, captured administrative credentials, and pushed the ransomware from the company’s own domain controllers to every joined machine at once. Email, computers, and business systems were lost at all three companies. The chemical companies’ plants, on separate networks, ran safely; some of Hydro’s plants stopped for lack of connection to production systems, and the company worked by hand, from paper and stock, with retirees who knew the old ways. Hydro isolated every plant, restored from backups, and paid no ransom. The attackers were criminals acting for payment, and law enforcement in several countries has investigated them. The lessons are that an enterprise domain can reach every plant at once, that plants must be able to run without the center, and that backups must be beyond an attacker’s reach. Any company that runs many sites from one network has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this company does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
