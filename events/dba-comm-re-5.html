Hurricane Maria, September 2017

Event date: from 20 September 2017 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

Hurricane Maria crossed Puerto Rico on 20 September 2017 as a high-end Category 4 storm, knocking down most of the island's utility poles and all its transmission lines. The day after landfall about 95 percent of reported cell sites were out, and sites then ran on generators for months while the grid was rebuilt, on fuel that was scarce and sometimes stolen. Broadcasting was largely off the air, 911 centers answered without full caller location, and it took six months for the cell sites to return to about 4 percent out.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: a natural-hazard event, a major hurricane that took down the communications network and the grid beneath it together, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, The storm and the grid were one event. Damage and the loss of power took the sites together, power's share growing as the weeks went on.
  • F-2, The design outage of a major hurricane can be months. The grid was down for months, and the network's restoration took six.
  • F-3, Endurance was the fuel that could be delivered and defended. Sites ran on generators for months, and their endurance was the fuel that could reach them and be kept from theft.
  • F-4, Surviving coverage is stated with its base and its population. The share of sites out was reported on a moving base, and did not say how many people were served.
  • F-5, Backhaul on the grid's poles fails with the grid. The fiber connecting sites hung on the utility's poles and came down with them.
  • F-6, The grid's restoration must hold the network's priorities and protect its plant. The utility's crews, without the carriers' priorities, removed newly installed fiber as debris.
  • F-7, The 911 centers answered, without the location the access network delivers. Calls were answered without full caller location and call-back numbers.
  • F-8, Broadcasting carried the area, and did not report its own state. Most stations were off the air, and the regulator learned their state by scanning the spectrum.
  • F-9, The network could not report its own state. Providers could not report because their communications were down, and the response worked without knowing what was up.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of cell sites, wireline and cable networks, broadcasters, and 911 centers in places exposed to major storms, and for the governments that coordinate their restoration. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose sites depend on a grid that a storm could take down for months. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Plan for the Storm and the Grid as One Event, Lasting Months

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads; the Federal Communications Commission’s report and the Government Accountability Office’s analysis are its record. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2: treat a major hurricane and the loss of the grid as one event, and size your sites’ endurance and your restoration plan to a grid outage of months; on Puerto Rico’s main island most of the grid was down for about two months, and the cell sites took six months to return to about 4 percent out.

A.3.2 Secure the Fuel and the Backhaul

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-5. Plan how fuel will reach your generators when ports and roads are closed and others control the supply, and how both will be protected from theft; and know which of your backhaul runs on the grid’s poles, with a path, such as satellite, that does not.

A.3.3 Coordinate Restoration and Report Your State

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4, F-6, F-7, F-8, and F-9. Agree your restoration priorities with the electric utility before the storm, and protect your restored plant from its crews; keep 911 location and call-back working when the network is degraded; keep a way to report your network’s state that does not depend on the network, and expect broadcasters to report theirs; and count surviving coverage by people served, against a stable base.

A.4 For the Board

Hurricane Maria crossed Puerto Rico on 20 September 2017 as a high-end Category 4 storm, two weeks after Hurricane Irma. It knocked down most of the island’s utility poles and all its transmission lines, and with them the fiber that connected cell sites. The day after landfall about 95 percent of reported cell sites were out of service, and in nearly two-thirds of the island’s municipalities every site was out. Most of the main island’s grid stayed down for about two months, and the sites ran on generators fed by fuel that was scarce, stolen, and at times controlled by others. The utility’s crews, working without the carriers’ priorities, removed newly installed fiber as debris. Broadcasting was largely off the air, the 911 centers answered without full caller location, and providers could not even report their own state. It took six months for the cell sites to return to about 4 percent out. The lessons are that a major storm and the loss of the grid are one event, lasting months, and that the network’s endurance is the fuel that can be delivered and defended. Any operation exposed to major storms has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
