The BP Texas City Refinery Explosion, Texas City, Texas, 23 March 2005

Event date: 23 March 2005 (record open as to one recommendation) · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Lifelines collection

What happened

During the startup of the isomerization unit at BP's Texas City refinery, operators overfilled a distillation tower for more than three hours while its level gauge showed the level falling and a backup alarm stayed silent. Relief valves discharged into a drum and stack open to the air, the liquid thrown out formed a vapor cloud, and its ignition killed fifteen workers, all in or near trailers sited near the unit, and injured about 180. 43,000 people sheltered in place. The refinery's later long outage followed Hurricane Rita and an overhaul.

Tier

No adversary established. Non-adversarial process-safety event: none of the four characteristics present.

Findings

  • F-1, The class's account is corrected. All fifteen died in or near trailers, the damage reached buildings and tanks around the unit, and the refinery's long outage followed Hurricane Rita and an overhaul.
  • F-2, The consequence is set by where people are. Every one of the fifteen dead was in or near a trailer close to the unit; the class counts units and production, not people.
  • F-3, Operators were misled by their own instruments. The level gauge read falling as the tower filled and the backup alarm stayed silent, with no adversary present; the class treats false data only as an attacker's work.
  • F-4, Startup is where the case lives. The explosion came in a startup run by fatigued operators with no supervisor present; the class names neither startup nor staffing.
  • F-5, Relief to the open air becomes a release. The relief valves emptied into a stack open to the air, and the liquid it threw out became the vapor cloud; the class does not name relief systems.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for owners and operators of petroleum refineries and of other plants that start up process units after turnarounds, the contractors who work beside those units, and the communities beside the plants. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to a refinery. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions a refinery answers about its own refinery (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account a refinery can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

On the Board’s recommendations, the American Petroleum Institute issued RP 753 on the siting of portable buildings, which bars lightweight wooden trailers from hazardous areas and limits nonessential occupants of portable buildings nearby; RP 754 on process safety performance indicators; RP 755 on fatigue, which limits work hours and consecutive days; and the sixth edition of RP 521, which requires inherently safer alternatives to atmospheric blowdown where a vapor cloud explosion is a risk. OSHA’s Process Safety Management standard (29 CFR 1910.119) governs refineries, and the agency ran a national emphasis program for refineries on the Board’s recommendation. The Panel’s ten recommendations to the company address process safety leadership, expertise, accountability, performance indicators, and board monitoring. Each is its author’s.

A.3.2 Prove the Instruments Before Startup, and Assess Organizational Change

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-4; the second follows the Board’s one open recommendation. Repair known instrument faults and prove the alarms before a unit starts up, and investigate earlier readings beyond range; and assess any change in staffing, budget, or ownership for its effect on process safety, whether or not a regulation yet requires it.

A.4 For the Board

On 23 March 2005, at the BP refinery in Texas City, operators starting up the isomerization unit after maintenance filled a tall distillation tower with flammable liquid for more than three hours. Its level gauge showed the level falling while it rose, and a backup alarm did not sound. The rising pressure opened relief valves into a drum and stack open to the air; liquid erupted from the stack, the vapor was ignited, most likely by an idling pickup truck, and the explosion killed fifteen workers, all of them in or near trailers placed close to the unit, and injured about 180. Forty-three thousand people sheltered indoors. Investigators found instruments whose repair had been put off, years of overfilled startups that no one had investigated, fatigued crews, and a decision in 2002 not to connect the drum to a flare. No one intended any of it. The lessons are that people must not be placed where an explosion can reach them, that instruments must be proved before a startup, and that relief systems must not send flammable material into the open air. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own refinery, and most of the answers cost little to find.
