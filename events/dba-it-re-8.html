The Attack on Dyn's Managed Name Service, 21 October 2016

Event date: 21 October 2016 (record closed) · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

Two distributed denial-of-service attacks, from about 11:10 to 13:20 and 15:50 to 17:00 UTC, flooded Dyn's managed name platform from a Mirai botnet of up to 100,000 internet-of-things devices on the provider's estimate. The names of many large services could not be resolved by users without held answers; holders with a second provider rode through. No government attribution is recorded.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. Adversarial, below the tier: intentionality and denial established, persistence not established, precision not determined.

Findings

  • F-1, A Single Point of Failure No Holder Chose. The sector knows where its names are concentrated.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for organizations whose essential services or operations depend on public names, the providers that answer for them, and those who oversee them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to an organization. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an organization answers about its own names (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an organization can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one organization. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the organization; it is that organization’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

The provider’s analysis sets out the attacks and its own response. The class’s requirements (§4.6, §5.3, §6.4) state names answered by more than one provider, answers held for a ride-through period, more than one resolver, and no control or protective path that needs a public name as it acts. Each is its author’s.

A.3.2 Find Out Who Answers for the Names You Depend On

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1. Find out which provider answers for your names and for the names of the services you depend on, and where many share one provider alone, treat it as a single point of failure in your own plan, whether or not any one of them chose it.

A.4 For the Board

On 21 October 2016, a company called Dyn, which runs the internet’s address lookup for many large websites and services, was flooded with traffic from tens of thousands of hacked household devices such as cameras and recorders. For several hours that day, people across the United States and Europe could not reach many well-known sites, because their computers could not look up where those sites were. The sites themselves were working. Companies that used a second lookup provider, and computers that had remembered the answers, were not affected. The lessons are that many organizations relying on the same provider can share a weakness none of them chose, and that every critical service should have more than one way to be found. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own names, and most of the answers cost little to find.
