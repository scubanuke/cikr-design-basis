The Destruction of the Kakhovka Dam, June 2023

Event date: February 2022 to June 2023 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

Russian forces took the Kakhovka dam on Ukraine's Dnipro River in February 2022 and held it for some fifteen months, during which its owner could neither operate nor inspect it. The reservoir was drawn low and then raised high, water poured over the spillway for six weeks, and the dam was breached in the early hours of 6 June 2023. About 620 square kilometers flooded, about 100,000 residents were directly affected, and about 700,000 people lost access to drinking water.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Placed at the military-action tier, on the author's ruling of 17 November 2026. Military action by a party to an armed conflict that held the dam, against the dam and the reservoir it impounds. Precision is read from the pattern of what was reached and kept from restoration: the one structure whose breach floods the valley and empties the reservoir others depend on, its pool driven to a three-decade low and then over the spillway, its damage left unrepaired, and its breach at the high pool. Ukraine and Russia each say the other destroyed the dam; no finding is made between them.

Findings

  • F-1, A held dam's pool is the holder's, not the permit's. The party holding the dam set its pool for months.
  • F-2, Persistence acts through operation, and a denied structure is still observed. Damage was visible from satellites days before the breach.
  • F-3, The design basis does not wait on attribution. The cause is disputed, and the people downstream needed a warning anyway.
  • F-4, The warning must reach a valley under another authority. Most of the flooded area lay in territory held by the party that held the dam.
  • F-5, A dependent's margin is its reserve and the time to build another. The Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant lost the reservoir it drew from.
  • F-6, When the dam is held, the owner's lever is upstream. The owner could act only through the dams above.
  • F-7, The consequence is validated on the footprint, not the count of the dead. Counts of the dead differ by an order of magnitude.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for owners and operators of dams with large reservoirs, the authorities that warn the valleys below them, and the facilities that depend on their pools, among them nuclear plants and water systems. It is also for any owner whose structure could be held or denied by another party in war. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Plan for a Held Dam, and Watch It From Outside

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1, F-2, and F-6: plan for the case in which a dam is held by another party, naming the pool that would make a breach worst and the levers upstream that the owner still holds; and watch a denied structure from outside it.

A.3.2 Warn the Whole Valley, Whatever the Cause

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3, F-4, and F-7. Act on the condition of the dam without waiting for attribution; make the warning reach every community in the footprint, including any under another authority; and validate the consequence on the footprint flooded, not on the count of the dead.

A.3.3 Know the Dependents’ Margins

This step is this instrument’s, from F-5. For each facility that draws on the pool, know its reserve and the time to build another source, so that the margin is stated before it is needed.

A.4 For the Board

Russian forces took the Kakhovka dam on Ukraine’s Dnipro River in the first days of the full-scale invasion of February 2022 and held it for some fifteen months, during which its owner could neither operate nor inspect it. The reservoir was drawn to a three-decade low and then raised to a three-decade high, water poured over the spillway for six weeks, and damage was visible on satellite images days before the dam was breached in the early hours of 6 June 2023. About 620 square kilometers flooded and about 100,000 residents were directly affected; about 700,000 people lost access to drinking water, and the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant upstream lost the reservoir it drew from and was cooled from its own pond. Ukraine and Russia each say the other destroyed the dam, and no court has ruled; this instrument makes no finding between them. The count of the dead differs by an order of magnitude among publishers. The lessons are that a dam held by another party is a dam whose pool and condition the owner must still plan for and watch, and that the warning must reach the whole valley whoever caused the breach. Any owner of a dam that could be held or denied has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this dam does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
