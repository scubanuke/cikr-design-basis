The Ammonia Release at Millard Refrigerated Services, August 2010

Event date: 23 August 2010, read to the settlement of 2 June 2015 (record closed) · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

On 22 August 2010 a cold-storage terminal near Mobile, Alabama, lost power for more than seven hours. During the restart an operator cleared an alarm, which interrupted a blast freezer's defrost, and a programming error let four coils grouped to one valve set go straight back to refrigeration full of hot gas. On the morning of 23 August the hydraulic shock ruptured a 12-inch suction pipe on the roof and released about 32,000 pounds of anhydrous ammonia. The cloud crossed a canal to contractors working on the Deepwater Horizon response; about 150 people reported exposure, 32 were hospitalized and four placed in intensive care. The emergency shutdown was not used, and the release ran about four hours.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: a latent control-logic error opened by an ordinary command during recovery from a power outage. None of the four characteristics is present.

Findings

  • F-1, The shutdown was available and not ordered. Name in advance who orders the emergency shutdown and on what detected release.
  • F-2, A local command took tested logic outside what was tested. The determinism showing must cover every command the controller accepts, local resets and overrides included.
  • F-3, The release was set by what one command could release. Postulate the release at the largest inventory one valve set or command can bring into play.
  • F-4, Erratum: the earlier releases. The agency attributes the 2007 release to hydraulic shock and identifies no root cause for January 2010.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owners and operators of cold-storage warehouses and other facilities with ammonia refrigeration, for the contractors who program and service their controls, and for the emergency planners of the communities around them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to a facility. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions a facility answers about its own plant (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account a facility can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Lessons and Requirements Already Published

The Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board’s bulletin gives five lessons: avoid grouping several large evaporators to one set of control valves; program the defrost sequence to depressurize or bleed the coil automatically on restart; avoid interrupting evaporators by hand while in defrost; run pump-out long enough to remove all liquid from the coils; and use the emergency shutdown to de-energize pumps, compressors, and valves in a release. The Environmental Protection Agency’s settlement records the Clean Air Act’s general duty to identify the hazard of hydraulic shock and design against it, and the risk-management program rule for plants above its threshold. Each is its publisher’s.

A.3.2 Name the Shutdown, Lock the Logic, and Count the Largest Release

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 to F-3. Write down who orders the emergency shutdown and on what detected release, so that it is pressed before anyone climbs to the roof. Test the defrost and restart logic for every command the controller accepts, an alarm reset and a manual override included, in every state it can be in after an outage, and restrict and record who can change it, at the panel as well as remotely. And size your release planning to the most hot gas and liquid that one valve set or one command can put into a line at once.

A.4 For the Board

On the morning of 23 August 2010, the refrigeration plant of a cold-storage terminal near Mobile, Alabama, ruptured and released about 32,000 pounds of ammonia. The plant had lost power for more than seven hours the day before. During the restart, an operator cleared an alarm, which interrupted the defrost of a blast freezer, and a programming error in the control system let the freezer’s coils go straight back to cooling while still full of hot gas. The shock that followed broke a large pipe on the roof. The cloud drifted across a canal to where contractors were working on the Gulf oil spill; about 150 people reported being exposed and 32 were hospitalized. The plant’s emergency shutdown was not used while staff tried to close valves by hand, and the release ran for about four hours. The lessons are that control logic is only as safe as the situations it has been tested in, a restart after an outage above all; that pressing the shutdown must be a decision made in advance, not in the cloud; and that how a plant is piped decides how bad its worst day is. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own plant, and most of the answers cost little to find.
