The Closure of the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz, from November 2023

Event date: from 19 November 2023, open on the record of 9 October 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

From November 2023, Houthi forces attacked merchant ships in the southern Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, seizing one and sinking four, and most shipping lines diverted around the Cape of Good Hope. After the strikes on Iran of 28 February 2026, the Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed to most shipping by the threat of attack and the withdrawal of insurance, and thousands of seafarers and hundreds of ships were held inside the Gulf. The main pipeline around the strait delivered into the Red Sea, where Saudi shipping was blockaded and the pipeline was struck; neither passage had recovered on the record of October 2026.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Military action, on the accounts of the actors and governments concerned: an armed campaign against shipping by the Houthi forces, and the closure of a strait in armed conflict, declared by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. Each attribution is stated as the government's or actor's own, and this instrument makes no finding of its own. The record is open.

Findings

  • F-1, A passage closed by threat may reopen obstructed. Wrecks and reported mines mean that a passage's closure runs past the end of the threat.
  • F-2, Severance time runs to the return of the ships, and it relapses. Traffic stayed far below normal long after a halt, and both passages relapsed after partial reopenings.
  • F-3, A passage is closed to some ships, and the country counts its own. Both passages were closed to ships chosen by association, so exposure should be counted for the ships a country depends on.
  • F-4, The military-mobility flow stops at the first attack on the flag. The carrier of United States government cargo suspended its Red Sea transits at the first such attack.
  • F-5, Withdrawn. Its finding on ships and seafarers held inside a closed sea is referred to the classes that hold them.
  • F-6, An alternative through the other passage is not independent. The bypass around one passage delivered into the other, which the same conflict reached.
  • F-7, The stranded flow states what it counts. Counts differed by an order of magnitude, so each must say what it measures and how.
  • F-8, Withdrawn. Its finding on dependent sectors' stocks is referred to those sectors' series.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its finding on navigation interference and radio orders at the chokepoint is referred to the communications series and the control class.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of ports, shipping lines, and other transportation nodes and corridors, and for the importers and dependent sectors, energy, fertilizer, and manufacturing among them, whose goods move through a foreign chokepoint. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose supply depends on a passage it does not control. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Count Your Own Ships, in Your Own Units

This step is this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-7. Count your exposure for the ships you depend on, by flag, ownership, cargo, and destination, and treat the passage as closed to them where an adversary has named their flag or associations, whatever share of its traffic still moves. State that exposure in barrels, tons, or containers, with the source, period, and baseline of every count, and know when a count rests on ships’ own position broadcasts.

A.3.2 Check That Your Alternative Is Independent

This step is this instrument’s, from F-6. Do not count as independent an alternative that delivers into another chokepoint or that the same campaign can reach; the main bypass around the Strait of Hormuz delivered into the Red Sea, which was then closed to Saudi shipping. State the alternative’s capacity with the fleet that would carry it, and its added time and cost as measured.

A.3.3 Plan for a Long Closure That Relapses

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2, with the substance of F-5 and F-8, which are referred to the classes and series that hold them. Plan for a closure that runs from the first threat to the return of shipping lines and insurers at a stated share of normal traffic, not to a ceasefire, with relapses counted and the clearance of mines and wrecks after the threat ends; Suez traffic was still 39 percent below its pre-crisis level ten months after the Houthi forces halted their attacks. Size stocks of every commodity a passage carries against that duration, and plan for ships and crews that may be held on the far side.

A.3.4 Treat Positions and Radio Orders as Possibly False

On 28 February 2026, the Joint Maritime Information Center advised that radio hails from Iranian Revolutionary Guard elements indicating transit restrictions did not constitute a legally recognized suspension of transit passage under international maritime law; ship operators follow the maritime security advisories in force. This step is this instrument’s, from the substance of F-9, which is referred to the communications series and to the control class: treat satellite positions and orders received over open channels near a chokepoint as indications that can be false, and know how your ships and your own staff would detect it.

A.4 For the Board

Since November 2023, the Houthi forces have attacked merchant ships in the southern Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, seizing one and sinking four, and most shipping lines have sent their ships the long way around Africa, about ten days longer between Asia and Europe. After the strikes on Iran of 28 February 2026, the Strait of Hormuz, which carried nearly a fifth of the world’s oil supply on the Energy Information Administration’s account, was effectively closed to most shipping, by the threat of attack and the withdrawal of insurance rather than by any physical blockage. Thousands of seafarers and hundreds of ships were held inside the Gulf. The pipeline that was meant to carry Saudi oil around the strait delivers into the Red Sea, where Saudi shipping was then blockaded and the pipeline itself was struck. On the record of October 2026, neither passage had recovered, and each had relapsed after a partial reopening. The lessons are that a passage closed to the ships you depend on is closed, whatever its average traffic; that an alternative which runs through another passage the same conflict can reach is no alternative; and that closures of this kind are measured in years, not weeks. Any operation that depends on goods moving through a passage it does not control has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
