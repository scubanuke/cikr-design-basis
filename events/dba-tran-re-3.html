The Power Outage at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, December 2017

Event date: 17 December 2017 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On the afternoon of 17 December 2017, the electric utility's switchgear failed and caught fire in a tunnel beneath the airport's concourses, where the cables of the airport's two supplies and the switch between them ran together. The terminals lost power shortly after one o'clock, flights were stopped, and the people inside were held for about five hours without public address or escalators before being evacuated. Power returned to all concourses around midnight, and more than a thousand flights were cancelled.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: the city's joint investigation found no evidence of foul play. It is an accidental failure of utility equipment inside the airport's fence, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, Separate at the source, shared on the way. The two supplies came from separate substations but met in one place, so independence must be shown along the whole route to the load.
  • F-2, The switchover time when the transfer is lost. With the switch itself destroyed there was no switchover, only repair, and the design basis should state that time too.
  • F-3, The node's power, owned by the utility inside the fence. The airport did not hold the design of the utility equipment inside its own fence, and that knowledge should be exchanged both ways, in writing.
  • F-4, Public address and vertical circulation at the point of use. Backup power lit the halls but did not carry the public address or the means of moving people who cannot use stairs.
  • F-5, Held before cleared, by several holders. The people inside were held for hours before being cleared, so the design basis should state how long a full node may hold its occupants and who decides to clear it.
  • F-6, The channel to the people the node holds. The airport judged its means of reaching passengers insufficient, and a channel that shares nothing with normal power and networks should be required.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its finding that recovery runs to the return of traffic, not of power, is carried by DBA-TRAN-RE-4, The Closure of Heathrow Airport.
  • F-8, The adversary need choose only the place where supplies meet. The design basis should treat the point where supplies converge, not only each supply, as the place to protect.
  • F-9, The gap the class fills, confirmed in the United States. No federal requirement obliges backup power for the passenger areas of an airport.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of airports, stations, terminals, and other transportation nodes that hold many people and take their power from a utility, above all where the utility owns or designs power equipment inside the node’s fence. It is also for any owner, in any sector, of a large building whose occupants depend on its power to be directed or moved. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Trace Every Supply to the Load

This step is this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-3. Walk each supply’s whole route from its source to the critical loads, including any length another party owns inside your fence, and show separately each place where two routes, or a route and the transfer between them, come together. Exchange with the utility, in writing, the routes and switching it runs inside your fence and the critical loads you need it to know.

A.3.2 Plan for the Day Both Supplies Are Lost

This step is this instrument’s, from F-2. State how long the site would be without power if a supply were lost together with its transfer equipment, as a time to restore by repair, counting the hours before the damaged equipment can be reached through fire, smoke, and fumes; at Atlanta those were nearly four, and power took about six hours to the first concourse and about eleven to the rest.

A.3.3 Power the Voice and the Way Out

No authority has published an action for owners from this event; the Government Accountability Office found in 2023 that the Federal Aviation Administration does not require backup power for the areas of an airport serving travelers, and the remedy at Atlanta, permanent backup generation for every terminal and concourse, came by settlement. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4 and F-5: carry the public address and the means of moving people who cannot use stairs on point-of-use supplies, as well as the emergency lighting; state how long the building can hold its occupants in place; and name in advance who decides to hold or to clear it.

A.3.4 Reach the People You Hold

This step is this instrument’s, from F-6. Keep a means of reaching the people inside that shares nothing with the building’s normal power and networks, and bring the utility and any company that operates your terminals into the site’s common plan.

A.4 For the Board

On the afternoon of 17 December 2017, electrical equipment owned by the local utility failed and caught fire in a tunnel beneath the concourses of Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport. The airport’s two supplies came from separate substations, but their cables and the switch between them ran together at that point in the tunnel, and the fire took them all. The terminals went dark shortly after one o’clock. Emergency lights came on, but the public address, the escalators, and security screening did not; the people inside were held for about five hours and then evacuated, and more than a thousand flights were cancelled. Utility crews could not reach the equipment for nearly four hours because of the fire and fumes, and power did not return to all the concourses until about midnight. Investigators found no evidence of foul play. The lesson does not depend on the cause. Two supplies from separate sources are only two if they stay separate all the way to the load, including the parts someone else owns, and backup power that lights the halls but cannot speak to the people in them leaves them waiting in the dark. Any building that holds many people and depends on its power to direct them has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
