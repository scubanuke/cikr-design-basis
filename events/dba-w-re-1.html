Water and Wastewater · Reference event · DBA-W-RE-1

The Minnesota Water Campaign, July and August 2026

Event date: 26 July to August 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.10 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 26 and 27 July 2026 a coordinated cyber attack disrupted controllers at more than thirty water and wastewater systems in Minnesota, shutting down one town's well and treatment plant for about two hours; by mid-August open-source analysis put the campaign at about one hundred facilities in at least twelve states. The attackers reconfigured internet-exposed controllers, many on cellular links, to lock operators out. An Iran-aligned group claimed responsibility; no official body has attributed the attacks, and the investigation is open.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. Not military action: three of the four characteristics are present and the fourth is absent, placing it at the upper bound of the adversarial tier.

Findings

  • F-1, Indication integrity is absent from the design basis. At another victim of the campaign, displays showed normal values while protection was disabled.
  • F-2, The defense-in-depth principle lacks a common-substrate test. Protection and indication were defeated through one controller project file.
  • F-3, The engineering workstation, vendor engineering software, and controller project file are not design basis objects. Legitimate engineering tools were used from outside.
  • F-4, The threat actor taxonomy does not accommodate capability proliferation. The techniques spread to many affiliated groups.
  • F-5, The applicability determination trigger now fires without discrimination. A small interior town was struck.
  • F-6, The scope threshold excludes a population that is materially exposed. The town struck serves fewer people than the framework's scope.
  • F-7, The framework depends on a single cyber reference event. Oldsmar had carried the whole cyber reference load.
  • F-8, Common third-party configuration is an unaddressed sector-level common substrate. Configuration shared by contractors could let one success repeat.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for drinking water and wastewater utilities of every size, especially small and rural systems without dedicated operational technology staff, and for the integrators and contractors who configure their controllers. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Take Control Technology Off the Internet

On 30 July 2026 the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency advised operators to remove water control technology from the public internet, and its announcement of that day gave explicit attention to end-of-life hardware and noted that impact depends on a utility’s capability to switch to manual operations. Its advisory AA26-097A documented the exploitation of internet-exposed programmable logic controllers. No step of this instrument’s is added here.

A.3.2 Make Indication and Protection Independent of the Controller

The announcement records that modified project files were found by noticing ladder logic discrepancies across several sites. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, and F-3: give operators a way to check the process that does not pass through the controller’s own displays; keep safety shutdowns and alarms on a substrate the controller project file cannot change; and treat the engineering workstation, vendor engineering software, and controller project files as protected assets, watching for their use from outside the organization.

A.3.3 Count Shared Configuration, and Plan the Manual Fallback

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-6 and F-8. Know whether a third party supplies a common configuration across you and other utilities, and treat that as a shared exposure; and make sure even the smallest system can run by hand and knows what a pressure loss means for its water.

A.3.4 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: this campaign reached controllers at towers, wells, and lift stations on cellular links, and a cellular modem usually takes its address from the carrier’s pool, so a search of the utility’s own blocks can come back clean while the controller is open. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element A-1 and Section 6.2.

A.4 For the Board

On 26 and 27 July 2026 a coordinated cyber attack disrupted controllers at more than thirty community water and wastewater systems across Minnesota; in Braham the attack shut down the well and treatment plant for about two hours, and Plymouth ran towers and lift stations by hand. By mid-August open-source analysis put the campaign at about one hundred facilities in at least twelve states, and near Atlanta a utility serving about 300,000 people issued a precautionary boil water advisory after cyber activity dropped its pressure. Officials said drinking water quality was not affected in the Minnesota cases. The attackers reached controllers exposed to the internet, many on cellular links and some past their support, and reconfigured them to lock operators out. The federal government had already reported, at another victim of the same campaign, a changed project file that disabled safety shutdowns and alarms while displays showed normal values. An Iran-aligned group claimed responsibility; no official body has attributed the attacks, and the investigation is open. The lessons are that control technology must come off the internet, that operators need a way to see the process that does not depend on the controller, and that protection must not stand on the same file as control. Any water utility has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this utility does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
