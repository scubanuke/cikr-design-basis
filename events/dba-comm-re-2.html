The Red Sea Cable Cuts, February 2024

Event date: 24 February 2024 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 24 February 2024, three subsea cable systems in the southern Red Sea were cut, most likely by the dragged anchor of a cargo ship that a Houthi missile had disabled six days earlier and its crew had abandoned. Traffic rerouted, and no country is reported to have lost its international connection, though some providers lost transit routes and saw added delay. The repair took about five months, almost all of it waiting on permits from two rival authorities, insurance, and security for the crew.

Tier

Military action, tier not established. Collateral severance under military action: the cables were cut in the course of a missile campaign against shipping, but the damage to them is not shown to have been intended, and their repair was denied for months by the conditions of the war. The accounts of the campaign and of who may have targeted the cables are each the speaker's own.

Findings

  • F-1, The surviving paths held, without designed priority. Eleven of fourteen systems remained and carried the traffic, by commercial redundancy rather than design.
  • F-2, A corridor at war is shared by the paths that survive in it. The paths that survived lay in the same sea and the same war.
  • F-3, A ship disabled by war is an initiating event. A ship disabled by a missile and abandoned cut the cables, and its wreck then stood in the way of repair.
  • F-4, Repair time was set by permission, not by the ship. A repair needing days of ship work waited about five months.
  • F-5, Repair can be denied by authority without force. Rival permit authorities and the terms of insurance and security delayed the repair without any attack on the repair ship.
  • F-6, Repair capacity in a war is one firm and one ship. One contractor and one ship repaired all three systems in turn.
  • F-7, The permits for a shared repair are coordinated. The three systems shared a contractor, a ship, and a permitting problem, and their permits should be coordinated.
  • F-8, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event was taken in by the class.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owners and operators of subsea cables, the carriers and consortia that share them, and the enterprises and governments whose international traffic depends on a corridor that runs through a region at war. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose links pass through waters where conflict may disable ships. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Count the Corridor at War as One Place

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2. Against conflict, count a corridor in which a war is being fought as one place for every path that runs through it, and keep paths outside it for the traffic that matters; test rerouting, and know its cost in latency and transit.

A.3.2 Plan for Repair That Is Denied by Permission

On 28 February 2024, the International Cable Protection Committee urged governments to expedite repair permits and to protect the vessels doing the work. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3, F-4, and F-5: count a ship disabled, abandoned, or sunk in a conflict near your corridor, and its wreck, as a cause of cuts and a hazard to repair; and plan in advance for the permits, insurance, and security a repair in contested waters needs, since in the Red Sea a repair that took days of ship work waited about five months.

A.3.3 Coordinate Repair Among Those Who Share It

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-6 and F-7. Know which contractor and ship your repair depends on and whether others depend on the same one, and coordinate permits and repair ships with the other carriers in your corridor and with the coastal authorities.

A.4 For the Board

On 24 February 2024, three subsea cable systems in the southern Red Sea, which carries most of the communications between Europe and Asia, were cut, most likely by the dragged anchor of a cargo ship that a missile had disabled six days earlier and its crew had abandoned. Traffic rerouted over other cables and overland, and no country is reported to have lost its international connection, though some providers lost transit routes and saw added delay. The repair needed a week to reach the cables and about two days of work on each, but it took about five months, waiting on permits from two rival authorities, a dispute over a consortium member, and insurance and security for a crew in a war zone. One contractor and one ship repaired all three systems in turn. The lessons are that paths that survive in the same war are not independent, that a ship disabled in a conflict can cut cables no one aimed at, and that repair in contested waters is set by permission, not by the ship. Any operation whose links run through waters at war has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
