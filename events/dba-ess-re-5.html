The Ransomware Attack on the City of Dallas, May 2023

Event date: 7 April to 11 May 2023 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

Early on 3 May 2023, ransomware launched across the City of Dallas's network took its computer-aided dispatch and the systems around it out of service, after the attackers had entered about four weeks earlier through a service account in the city's domain and taken about 1.169 terabytes of data. 911 calls were still answered, but dispatchers relayed calls by radio from handwritten notes, and units lost mobile data, station alerting, maps, and address history. Dispatch returned to full automation on 11 May, eight days later.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate criminal attack, not military action: the attackers were a criminal group seeking extortion, and dispatch was lost along with the city's other systems rather than chosen.

Findings

  • F-1, Separation of dispatch includes identity and administration. The attackers reached dispatch through the city's shared domain and remote-management tools.
  • F-2, Answering survived, again. 911 answering kept working when dispatch was lost.
  • F-3, Dispatch by hand covers what is lost with the software. Manual dispatch did not replace station alerting, mobile data, maps, or address history.
  • F-4, The cost of dispatch by hand is measured. The city and the unions gave different accounts of the effect, and no one measured it.
  • F-5, After a city-wide attack, plan for a week. Dispatch took eight days to return to full automation.
  • F-6, Data taken from the city is information about the response. The data taken included records bearing on the city's public-safety response.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its completion of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for 911 centers and the city and county technology teams that run computer-aided dispatch and the systems around it, mobile data, station alerting, maps, and records. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose operational system shares an identity domain with the rest of its organization. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Separate Dispatch by Identity, and Treat Taken Data as Exposure

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads; the city’s report is its record. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-6: separate dispatch from the rest of the organization by identity domain and administrative tools as well as by network; watch for an intruder who stays for weeks; and treat data taken from a network that holds public-safety records as information about the response.

A.3.2 Keep Answering Separate, Cover Every Lost Function, and Plan for a Week

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2, F-3, F-4, and F-5. Keep 911 answering independent of dispatch; make the manual mode cover each function lost with dispatch, station alerting, unit updates, maps, records, and address history; measure the times of manual call handling and dispatch; and plan for about a week without automated dispatch after a city-wide attack, as Dallas needed eight days.

A.4 For the Board

Early on 3 May 2023, ransomware launched across the City of Dallas’s network took its computer-aided dispatch and the systems around it out of service. On the city’s report, the attackers had entered about four weeks earlier through a basic service account in the city’s domain, moved through the network with legitimate remote-management tools, and taken an estimated 1.169 terabytes of data. 911 calls were still answered, but dispatchers relayed calls by radio from handwritten notes, and units lost mobile data, station alerting, maps, and the history of the addresses they were sent to. The city said the disruption was minimal; the unions said calls slipped through and units went to wrong locations; no one measured it. Dispatch returned to full automation eight days later. The attackers were a criminal group, not a state. The lessons are that dispatch must be separated from the rest of the city by identity as well as network, that manual operation must cover everything the software did, and that its cost must be measured. Any operation whose critical system shares accounts with the rest of the organization has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
