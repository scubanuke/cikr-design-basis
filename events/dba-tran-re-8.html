The Collapse of the Francis Scott Key Bridge, March 2024

Event date: 26 March 2024 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

In the early morning of 26 March 2024, the containership Dali lost power and steering about four minutes from the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore and struck one of its piers, bringing down the central spans. A call from the ship's pilot let police stop traffic about 48 seconds before the strike, but six members of a highway maintenance crew on the bridge were not warned and were killed. The channel beneath reopened in stages and was fully restored after seventy-six days; the replacement bridge is still being procured.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: the National Transportation Safety Board found the cause to be a loose wire aboard the ship, and officials saw no evidence of terrorism. It is an accidental vessel strike on a span over navigable water, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, The spans at risk must first be found. The bridge's risk of collapse from a ship strike was almost thirty times the criterion, and it had never been assessed.
  • F-2, Pier protection is credited by what it intercepts. The protection was rated in place and working, but the ship struck where it was not.
  • F-3, The assessment is renewed when the vessels change. Ships had grown about tenfold since the bridge's protection last stopped one.
  • F-4, The warning time begins at the loss of control, and its path is named. Traffic was stopped by a chain of calls with seconds to spare, and the path from ship to closure should be named and fast.
  • F-5, The workers on the span are reached apart from the closure. The six workers who died were not warned, and reaching people on a span must be a separate, exercised step.
  • F-6, The channel's repair is counted in stages, and its capacity is stated. The channel reopened in stages, and the salvage capacity it depended on should be known in advance.
  • F-7, Replacement time is the time to build the bridge current standards require. The replacement's estimate more than doubled once it was designed to current standards.
  • F-8, An alternative absorbs only what it admits. The harbor tunnels could not take hazardous materials, which had no crossing at all.
  • F-9, In the deliberate case no one calls. The warning here came from the ship itself, which a design basis for a deliberate strike cannot count on.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the owners of bridges over navigable water and of other critical spans, for the port and channel authorities beneath them, and for the pilots and vessel traffic services whose word may be the first warning. It is also for any owner, in any sector, of a structure that large moving vessels or vehicles pass close to. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Find and Assess the Spans at Risk

The American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials recommends a vessel-collision vulnerability assessment of such bridges, and the National Transportation Safety Board, in its report MIR-25-10, named 68 bridges held by 30 owners that had no recent assessment and asked their owners to make one. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, and F-3: credit pier protection only for what it is shown to intercept and stop, as built and for the ships that use the channel now, and renew the assessment whenever the size, displacement, or number of those ships changes, and at a stated interval in any case.

A.3.2 Warn and Clear Everyone on the Span

The Board recommended motorist warning systems on such bridges (H-25-28 to H-25-30) and emergency communications for the workers on them (H-25-31). These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-4 and F-5: name every link of the path by which a warning reaches the means of closing the span, starting from the loss of control aboard; let the first party to receive the warning close the span; and clear the people on the span as a step separate from closing the lanes, staffed so that neither waits on the other, exercised and timed.

A.3.3 Know Your Recovery Before You Need It

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-6, F-7, and F-8. State the salvage, heavy-lift, and wreck-removal capacity your channel would need, where it would come from, and how long it would take to arrive; estimate in advance the time and cost of a replacement built to today’s standards; and know which cargo, by class, has no other crossing.

A.4 For the Board

In the early morning of 26 March 2024, the containership Dali lost electrical power and steering about four minutes from the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore and struck one of its piers. The pier and the central spans collapsed. A call from the ship’s pilot let police stop traffic about 48 seconds before the strike, but six members of a highway maintenance crew on the bridge were never warned and were killed. The National Transportation Safety Board found that the bridge had never been assessed for the risk of a ship strike, that its annual risk of collapse was almost thirty times the accepted criterion, and that its pier protection was not where the ship hit; ships using the channel had grown about tenfold since a smaller one was stopped by that protection in 1980. The Board found the cause to be a loose wire aboard the ship, and officials saw no sign of an attack. The channel took seventy-six days to reopen fully, and the replacement is now estimated at more than twice the first estimate, with no final schedule. The lessons are that a structure built for the ships of its day must be reassessed for the ships of today, and that the people on it must be warned by a path that does not depend on a phone call reaching the right person in time. Any owner whose structure stands near large moving vessels or vehicles has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
