The KA-SAT Attack, February 2022

Event date: 24 February 2022 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

At the start of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, an attacker who had entered the management network of the KA-SAT satellite broadband service the evening before used legitimate management commands to overwrite the memory of tens of thousands of customer modems in about forty-five minutes. Customers in Ukraine and across Europe lost service, and about 5,800 wind turbines in central Europe lost remote monitoring and control for weeks while continuing to run. Malicious traffic attacked the restoration for weeks.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Military action: a cyber attack through the management plane of a satellite network at the onset of an armed conflict. The European Union and the governments of the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia, and Estonia attributed it to Russia, each in its own words; this instrument makes no finding of attribution.

Findings

  • F-1, The reach was set by the management partition. One set of commands reached every terminal in one partition across a continent.
  • F-2, The way in was the operators' remote access. The attacker came in through the operators' own remote access, not an unknown vulnerability.
  • F-3, Legitimate commands need a structural limit. The commands were legitimate in form, so only a structural limit on what one command can reach would have held.
  • F-4, Restoring a terminal fleet takes weeks. Disabled terminals were updated, reset, or replaced over about eight weeks.
  • F-5, The adversary attacked the restoration. Malicious traffic was renewed in new forms for weeks to keep terminals offline.
  • F-6, Dependents reached through distributors are not on the operator's map. Customers such as wind-farm operators reached the service through distributors the operator did not map.
  • F-7, The dependent kept working; its second path must share no management. The turbines ran on, but many wind farms had no backup link for monitoring.
  • F-8, The act reached beyond its aim. An attack aimed at Ukraine reached consumers in at least six other countries.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of satellite and other access networks that manage large fleets of customer terminals remotely, for the distributors that sell their service, and for the enterprises, such as wind-farm operators, whose equipment depends on those terminals. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose devices can all be reached by one management system. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Guard the Way Into the Management Plane

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the Federal Bureau of Investigation, in advisory AA22-076A of 17 March 2022, recommended that satellite communications providers use multifactor authentication, least privilege, the removal of unnecessary trust relationships, and the monitoring of unusual login attempts. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-2: treat the remote access by which your operators reach the management plane as the door an attacker will use, and keep the system that distributes software to terminals separate from it.

A.3.2 Put a Structural Limit on What One Command Can Reach

This step is this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-3. Divide your terminal fleet so that no one set of management commands can reach more of it than you can afford to lose, and make the distribution system structurally unable to push a change to more than that, however legitimate the command appears.

A.3.3 Plan to Restore a Fleet Under Attack

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4 and F-5. Plan to restore disabled terminals by update, reset, and replacement over weeks, with spares held by you or your critical customers; and plan for an adversary who attacks the restoration as well as the network.

A.3.4 Know Your Dependents, Wherever They Are

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-6, F-7, and F-8. Know which critical dependents reach your service through distributors; for dependents, keep a second path that shares no management with the first; and treat terminals far from a conflict as within its reach if they share management with terminals in it.

A.3.5 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the way in on this record was the remote access by which operators reach the management network, and the device that answers it is easily left off the list of the network it guards. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element I-1 and F-2.

A.4 For the Board

At the start of Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, an attacker who had entered the management network of the KA-SAT satellite broadband service the evening before used legitimate management commands to overwrite the memory of tens of thousands of customer modems in about forty-five minutes. The European Union and the governments of the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia, and Estonia, each in its own words, attributed the attack to Russia, the European Union stating that it facilitated the military aggression. The attack also reached customers across Europe, and about 5,800 wind turbines in central Europe lost remote monitoring and control for weeks, though they kept running. Malicious traffic attacked the restoration for weeks. The attacker came in through the operators’ own remote access, and from there reached the system that sends commands to every terminal. The lessons are that the management plane is the way to an entire fleet, that one command should never be able to reach more than you can afford to lose, and that an attack on one country can reach customers far from the conflict. Any operation whose devices are all managed from one place has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
