The Ransomware Attack on the Port of Nagoya, July 2023

Event date: 4 July 2023 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 4 July 2023, a ransomware attack encrypted every server of the one terminal operating system that runs all five container terminals and the central gate at the Port of Nagoya, Japan's first port by total cargo. The gate was shut for about three days; on the government committee's account the ships were worked by hand, while the operator's report says work stopped at every terminal. The system was restored from backups that themselves held the malware, and after the restored records were checked against the yard, the terminals restarted on 6 July.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. Not a state act on the record: a criminal ransomware attack, which press and government accounts attribute to a criminal group, each as its own statement. It took the one platform the port's terminals shared, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, No fallback at the gate, two accounts at the ship. The gate had no way to work by hand, so the fallback should be stated for each interface through which flow passes.
  • F-2, The fallback rested on people who remembered the work. Working by hand was possible only because staff who knew the work before the system were still there.
  • F-3, One platform for a port, owned by none of its users. Where several terminals share one system, who owns it and who holds its backups, access, and fallback should be settled by contract.
  • F-4, Backups that held the compromise. The backups were too short to reach back before the infection and covered only part of the system, so they carried the attack back in.
  • F-5, The channel the postulated event names and the requirement does not. The attackers likely entered through a maintenance connection reachable from anywhere with a password, and such channels should be restricted, protected, logged, and closed when not in use.
  • F-6, Restore first, a decision the class does not place. Who decides between restoring quickly and preserving evidence, and on whose advice, should be named in advance.
  • F-7, The records reconciled with the yard before work resumed. Checking restored records against the physical yard worked, and its method and duration belong in recovery planning.
  • F-8, Stranded flow, counted at the ship and the gate, and the dependent facility. Stranded flow should be measured at each interface so that dependents such as a manufacturer's packing lines can plan on real numbers.
  • F-9, The gap the class fills, partly closed elsewhere for the platform. Japan's later measures govern the security of terminal systems but set no fallback rate or limit on stranded flow.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of ports, container terminals, and other transportation nodes where several terminals or several companies work on one shared operating system, and for anyone who maintains such a system from outside. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose sites share one system that a contractor maintains remotely. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know Who Owns the Shared System

This step is this instrument’s, from F-3. Where several terminals or companies share one system, name its owner and maintainer, and record by contract who holds its backups, its external access, its fallback procedures, and their exercise.

A.3.2 Close the Maintenance Door

The committee that Japan’s Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism set up after the event set emergency measures for every terminal operating system, among them authentication by more than one factor for external access. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-5: restrict any remote-access channel to known sources, patch it on a stated interval, log it outside the system it reaches, and open it only while it is in use.

A.3.3 Keep Backups That Reach Back Before the Attack

The committee’s emergency measures include backups in several generations, kept apart from the system. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-4: keep backups that reach back beyond the longest time an intrusion might go undetected, that cover every environment needed to rebuild the system, and that are shown clean before anything is restored from them.

A.3.4 Plan for the System’s Failure, Not Only for Disaster

The committee’s emergency measures include a plan for system failure that covers manual working; in this event the operator had a plan for disasters and none for a system failure. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1, F-2, F-6, F-7, and F-8: state the rate each interface can sustain by hand and for how long, train staff in it so that it does not rest on those who worked before the system, name in advance who decides between restoration and preservation and on whose advice, time the check of restored records against the yard, and record the flow stranded at each interface after any loss.

A.3.5 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the door on this record was the channel used to maintain the system, reachable from any internet address, and a maintenance contractor’s equipment may sit at the contractor’s address rather than the operator’s. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element I-2 and F-5.

A.4 For the Board

On 4 July 2023, a ransomware attack stopped the one computer system that runs all five container terminals and the central gate at the Port of Nagoya, the busiest port in Japan by total cargo. On the reading of the government’s committee, the attackers came in through a maintenance connection that anyone on the internet with the right username and password could reach. Every server was encrypted, and the backups held only three days of data and carried the malware back in when the system was restored. The gate was shut for about three days. On the committee’s account the ships were still worked by hand, because people were on staff who had done the work before the system existed; the operator’s own report says work stopped at every terminal. About 20,000 containers were affected, and a major manufacturer stopped export packing lines. No government has attributed the attack to a state; press and government accounts name a criminal ransomware group. The lesson does not depend on who did it. One system served everyone, one door reached it, and the backup could not be trusted. Any operation where several sites or companies share one system maintained from outside has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
