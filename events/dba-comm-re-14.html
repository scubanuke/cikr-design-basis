The Salt Typhoon Intrusion, Disclosed 2024

Event date: disclosed from September 2024; activity since at least 2019 on the United States government's account · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

In September 2024 it became public that actors had compromised at least nine United States telecommunications carriers, taking call records, the communications of a limited number of people in government and politics, and information about court-ordered law-enforcement requests. They entered through publicly known flaws in routers and other network devices, changed those devices to stay, and were inside for one to two years or more on officials' accounts. Federal agencies advised senior officials to use end-to-end encrypted communications.

Tier

Military action, tier not established. State espionage, not military action: the United States government attributes the intrusion to actors sponsored by the government of the People's Republic of China, and that attribution is its own. The purpose was espionage, and no service was taken away. The record is open.

Findings

  • F-1, The network's own devices are a door to the operations layer. The actors came in through known flaws in routers and edge devices.
  • F-2, Administering intercept systems is an operations function. The systems built to answer lawful-intercept requests were reached and should be governed as operations.
  • F-3, Eviction is shown on evidence the adversary could not reach. Carriers said the actors were gone, but the logs had been erased and the assessments were not provided when asked for.
  • F-4, The commons is protected against being read. Officials were told to assume their mobile communications could be read and to use end-to-end encryption.
  • F-5, For a compromise, hunting is prepared in advance. The response was improvised, and its federal review ended before it reported.
  • F-6, The presence is measured, not the purpose. Access held for espionage is access available for disruption.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for telecommunications carriers and the operators of other networks whose routers and edge devices are reachable from outside, for the systems they keep to answer lawful-intercept requests, and for officials and others whose communications may be read. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose network devices could hold an adversary for years. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Close the Known Doors, and Protect the Intercept Systems

On 3 December 2024 the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, the National Security Agency, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation, with the cyber agencies of Australia, Canada, and New Zealand, issued hardening guidance to the defenders of communications infrastructure, and a joint advisory lists publicly known flaws in network devices that the actors used. On 15 January 2025 the Federal Communications Commission ruled that section 105 of the Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act requires carriers to secure their networks against unlawful access and interception; that binding interpretation was later withdrawn in favor of voluntary commitments. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1 and F-2: treat the management interfaces of your own routers and edge devices as a door into your operations, and administer the systems that answer lawful-intercept requests as an operations function with controls of their own.

A.3.2 Measure the Presence, and Show the Eviction

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-6. Design your detection for an adversary who stays for years and erases logs, and measure the presence, not its purpose, since access held for espionage is access available for disruption; and show that an intruder is gone on evidence the intruder could not have reached.

A.3.3 Protect What Is Said, and Prepare the Hunt

On 18 December 2024 the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency told highly targeted individuals in senior government and political positions to assume their mobile communications were at risk of interception or manipulation and to use only end-to-end encrypted communications. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-4 and F-5: protect the communications that matter against being read on a compromised network; and prepare in advance, with other carriers and the authorities, how a compromise across many networks will be hunted.

A.3.4 List What Talks to the Outside, Then Look as an Attacker Would

List first, then look. The inventory comes first because exposed equipment often sits outside your own address space: the doors on this record were the management interfaces of routers and edge devices, which are easily left off the inventory of the network they run. Then look at each address on the list as an attacker would, by any of three routes, each its provider’s. Shodan and Censys each offer a search of the devices they have found answering on the internet, and a search limited to your own address blocks and the addresses on your list shows you what it would show anyone. In the United States, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency offers Cyber Hygiene Vulnerability Scanning, which scans the internet-facing assets an organization names and reports weekly, at no cost to governments and critical infrastructure organizations (cisa.gov, Cyber Hygiene Services). Your integrator and vendors can tell you what they can reach remotely, if you ask them. Whatever the look finds that is not on your list goes on it, and whatever need not talk to the outside should stop. The inventory and the look are a standing item in every annex whose event turned on equipment reachable from the internet, on the author’s direction of 15 November 2026; here they rest on element I-1 and F-1.

A.4 For the Board

In September 2024 it became public that actors the United States government says were sponsored by the government of the People’s Republic of China had compromised at least nine United States telecommunications carriers, an intrusion known as Salt Typhoon. On the government’s account they took call records, the communications of a limited number of people in government and politics, and copies of information about court-ordered law-enforcement requests. They entered through publicly known flaws in routers and other network devices, one disclosed in 2018, changed the devices to stay, erased logs, and were inside for one to two years or more on officials’ accounts. The purpose was espionage, and no service was taken away. Federal agencies told senior officials to assume their mobile communications could be read and to use end-to-end encryption. Three carriers said the actors were gone, but in 2026 their security assessments had not been provided to the congressional member who asked for them. The lessons are that a network’s own devices are a door into its operations, that presence held for spying is presence available for disruption, and that an eviction must be shown, not claimed. Any operation whose network devices face the outside has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
