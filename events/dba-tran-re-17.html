The Chatsworth Collision, September 2008

Event date: 12 September 2008 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 12 September 2008, a Metrolink commuter train left Chatsworth station in Los Angeles, passed a red signal, and ran head-on into a Union Pacific freight train on single track; twenty-five people were killed. The signal system worked as designed and held the stop, but nothing stopped the train once its engineer, distracted by text messaging against company rules on the Board's finding, passed the signal. Congress required positive train control five weeks later, and it was in operation on every required route mile by the end of 2020.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: a person's passage of a stop correctly given, in territory with no enforcement, below the threshold for the military-action tier. The probable cause is the National Transportation Safety Board's.

Findings

  • F-1, The control plane granted the stop and held it. The signals and control logic did their job; the stop was correctly given and enforced in the field.
  • F-2, Chatsworth is a limit passed, not a proceed granted. No one gave the train permission to move; it passed the limit of the permission it had, a case that can be stopped only at the movement.
  • F-3, The enforcement the class requires is the enforcement the event needed. Positive train control would have stopped the train before it passed the signal.
  • F-4, The duration is a braking distance, not the time to the conflict. Enforcement must act before the point from which a train can no longer stop, not after the signal is passed.
  • F-5, Enforcement independence has two parts. Enforcement must be independent both of the person and of the signal system whose knowledge it enforces.
  • F-6, A check by a person or a rule is not a layer independent of the person. Every check on the engineer was another person or a rule, and none could stop the train.
  • F-7, The enforcement can still be absent, exempt, or cut out. Places and modes without active enforcement remain, and each should be treated as an accepted risk.
  • F-8, The indication must be legible where the person decides. The red signal could not reliably be read from the station where the train departed.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for railroads, passenger and freight, and their dispatch centers, and for transit systems whose trains are governed by signals a person must obey. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose operation relies on a person stopping where a signal says to stop. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Enforce the Stop Where People Can Fail

The Rail Safety Improvement Act of 2008 requires positive train control on the main lines it names (49 U.S.C. 20157); the Federal Railroad Administration’s rule requires each system to prevent train-to-train collisions and movement through a misaligned switch (49 CFR 236.1005), and the Administration announced it in operation on every required route mile on 29 December 2020. In 2023 the National Transportation Safety Board found limits that remain: terminal exceptions, limits in knowing where trains are during restricted-speed operation, and switching-mode software that can disable signal enforcement near a switching operation. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3, F-4, and F-7: make enforcement stop the train short of the limit, acting on its braking distance; treat every place and mode without active enforcement as a risk you have accepted on the record; make the return to active enforcement after a suspension an engineering control, as the Board recommended; and set the speed of a train running without enforcement by how far ahead its crew can read its signals.

A.3.2 Do Not Count People and Rules as Barriers

The Federal Railroad Administration restricted on-duty use of electronic devices by emergency order in October 2008 and by rule in 2010, and its rule of 12 October 2023 requires image recorders on passenger locomotives by 12 October 2027. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-6 and F-8: count repeat-backs, operating rules, device bans, efficiency tests, and recordings as monitoring, not as a layer that would stop a train; and make the indication a crew decides on legible where the decision is made, by an in-cab display or by signals shown to be readable from where trains stop.

A.3.3 Know What Your Enforcement Knows

This step is this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-5, with the note on cab entry that F-9 sends to Conveyances and Fleet. Know whether your enforcement learns where trains are only from the signal system it enforces, and credit it as independent of that system only to the extent it knows occupancy from another source; and make sure responders can enter your locomotive cabs quickly after a collision.

A.4 For the Board

On the afternoon of 12 September 2008, a Metrolink commuter train left Chatsworth station in Los Angeles, accelerated past a red signal, and ran head-on into a Union Pacific freight train on single track. Twenty-five people were killed. The signal system had done its job: the signal was red, and the control logic would not have let it show anything else. The National Transportation Safety Board found that the engineer, distracted by text messaging against company rules, did not respond to it, and that positive train control would have stopped the train. Every check on him was another person or a rule; the signal could not reliably be seen from the station, and nothing could stop the train once he passed it. Congress required positive train control five weeks later, and it was in operation on every required route mile by the end of 2020; in 2023 the Board found that gaps remain, in places it does not cover, in modes that switch it off, and in knowing where trains are. The lesson is that where a person can fail, only a layer that does not depend on that person will stop the movement, and every place where that layer is absent or switched off is a place where this can happen again. Any operation that relies on a person stopping where a signal says to stop has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
