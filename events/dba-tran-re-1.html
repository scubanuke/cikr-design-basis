The NotPetya Attack on Maersk's Terminals, June 2017

Event date: 27 June 2017 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On 27 June 2017, destructive malware reached the shipping company A.P. Moller-Maersk through an update to a Ukrainian tax program used in one of its offices, spread across the company's single global network, and destroyed the systems it reached. The company shut its systems down to contain it, and container terminals run by its terminal company on five continents closed or slowed, some for days, working by hand where they could while neighbors and partners took cargo. All affected terminals were reported open by 5 July, after the company rebuilt its computing in about ten days.

Tier

Military action, tier not established. An adversarial cyber attack, attributed to the Russian military by the United Kingdom and the United States, each stating that attribution as its own, and described by them as aimed at Ukraine. The terminals were not shown to be the target: they were reached because the company's network connected them to an office in Ukraine. This instrument makes no finding of attribution.

Findings

  • F-1, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.
  • F-2, The shared platform was the operator's own network. Every terminal shared one corporate network and identity domain, entered through software no terminal ran, and the terminals' own systems should be separated from it.
  • F-3, The shutdown is a decision on every node at once. Isolating a shared network to contain an attack stops every site that depends on it, so its holders and its scope should be planned in advance.
  • F-4, The manual fallback rate, observed, is many rates. Terminals ranged from no working at all to reduced productivity, so the rate by hand should be stated for each function, not once per site.
  • F-5, Diversion capacity needs its own information. Neighbors could take cargo only where the information to handle it reached them without the lost network.
  • F-6, Restoration rested on chance. The rebuild depended, on one published account, on a single copy of the company's network directory kept offline by a power cut, and restoration from protected copies should be required and exercised.
  • F-7, The node lost what it knew. Records were destroyed rather than altered, and those kept for working by hand should be current to a stated interval.
  • F-8, A node reached as the collateral of an attack aimed elsewhere. On the attributing governments' accounts the terminals were collateral of an attack on another country, a form the design basis should name.
  • F-9, The regulatory record has changed since the event. The Coast Guard's 2025 rule answers this event's path in controls but sets no fallback rate, restoration time, or limit on stranded flow.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of container terminals, ports, airports, rail yards, and other transportation nodes whose terminal operating systems, gates, and equipment controls run on a network the operator shares with the rest of its business. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose operating sites share one corporate network and one set of credentials with offices elsewhere. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Separate the Operating Systems From the Business Network

The Coast Guard’s rule on cybersecurity in the marine transportation system, in force since 16 July 2025, requires the owners and operators it covers to apply cybersecurity controls and to have cybersecurity plans in place by 16 July 2027 (33 CFR 101.650); for those owners, those are the published actions. This step is this instrument’s, from F-2: keep the terminal operating system, the gates, and the equipment controls on a network and an identity domain of their own, so that the loss of the business network does not take them, show that separation as built, and know which suppliers’ software shares the network they run on.

A.3.2 Decide the Shutdown Before You Need It

This step is this instrument’s, from F-3. Isolating a shared network to stop an attack may be the right decision, and it stops every site that depends on that network at once. Name in advance who holds that decision and to whom it passes when that person cannot be reached, plan the isolation so that it takes out the fewest sites the containment allows, and count the time it holds the sites down in their recovery time.

A.3.3 Work by Hand, and Hand Off Without the Network

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-4, F-5, and F-7. State for each site and each function the rate it can sustain by hand and for how long, and measure it in exercises. Keep the records you would work from current to a stated interval. Agree in advance with your carriers and neighboring terminals how load lists, bay plans, and release information would reach them without your network, as the carriers in this event had to find on the day.

A.3.4 Keep a Copy No Attacker Can Reach

The Coast Guard’s rule requires the owners and operators it covers to keep protected and frequently tested backups (33 CFR 101.650(g)). This step is this instrument’s, from F-6: make the systems your sites depend on, the identity domain among them, restorable from copies that no system an attacker could reach can alter or destroy, within a stated restoration time that is exercised end to end.

A.4 For the Board

On 27 June 2017, destructive malware, which the governments of the United Kingdom and the United States attributed to the Russian military and described as aimed at Ukraine, reached the shipping company A.P. Moller-Maersk through an update to a Ukrainian tax program used in one of its offices. It spread across the company’s single global network, and the company shut its systems down to contain it. Container terminals run by its terminal company on five continents closed or slowed, some for days, and worked by hand where they could; neighbors and partners took cargo where they could get the information to handle it. The company rebuilt thousands of servers and computers in about ten days, and that rebuild, on one published account, rested on a single copy of its network directory that a power cut had happened to keep offline. The terminals were not the target. They stopped because they shared a network and a set of passwords with an office in another country. Any operation whose sites share one network and one set of credentials with the rest of the business has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
