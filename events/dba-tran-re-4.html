The Closure of Heathrow Airport, March 2025

Event date: 20 to 22 March 2025 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

Just before midnight on 20 March 2025, a transformer failed and caught fire at the North Hyde substation in west London, and the fire took the substation's other supplies with it, cutting off one of the three points from which Heathrow Airport takes its power. The other two points kept their power, but each fed a separate part of the airport, and moving the lost load onto them took manual switching through most of the next day. The airport closed for most of 21 March, about 1,300 flights were cancelled, and full operation returned on 22 March.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: the police found no evidence that the incident was suspicious, and the review found the cause most likely to be moisture in a failed transformer component. It is an accidental equipment failure outside the airport's fence, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, Independent supplies that could not stand in for each other. Redundancy requires both that supplies share nothing and that each can carry the others' critical loads in time.
  • F-2, The switchover time, measured. The switchover took about eleven hours to the terminals and fourteen and a half to the whole network, and had never been exercised.
  • F-3, Power restored is not flow restored. Full operation came about twenty-eight hours after the loss, so recovery should be counted to the return of traffic.
  • F-4, The backup was built for safety, not for the switchover. Backup supplies would have run down before the switchover was done, and should be sized to it.
  • F-5, Knowledge at the utility is necessary, and buys no priority. The utility did not know the airport's internal arrangements, and even if it had, rules on equal treatment mean the airport must ride through on its own.
  • F-6, The decision to close held by the operator. The decision to close was rightly the airport's, and its holders, their succession, and reopening by part should be planned in advance.
  • F-7, Coordination with the utility was not prepared, and the scenario was not exercised. There was no agreed way to bring the parties together, and the slowest switchover should be exercised with the utility taking part.
  • F-8, The adversary need choose only the supply point slowest to switch. The design basis should be tested against the loss of the supply point that takes longest to switch over, and should not credit a supply as continuous on a judgment of how likely its loss is.
  • F-9, The gap the class fills, confirmed. The United Kingdom has no cross-sector requirement that critical operators keep operating through a loss of power.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of airports, ports, stations, and other transportation nodes fed from more than one supply point, where each supply point feeds a separate part of the site. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose site takes its power from several points that are not normally connected. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Make Sure Your Supplies Can Carry Each Other

The review by the National Energy System Operator recommends, in its eighth recommendation, that critical sites be able to switch load quickly between their supply points. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2: show that the critical loads of any one supply point can be carried by the others within a stated switchover time, by automatic transfer acting on the loss of voltage at the site where you can, and exercise that switchover end to end, from the loss to the last critical system restored. At Heathrow the plan estimated ten to twelve hours and the measured time was about eleven hours to the terminals and fourteen and a half to the whole network.

A.3.2 Size Backup Power to the Switchover

This step is this instrument’s, from F-4. Size the backup supplies of life-safety systems, and of the systems needed to hold people safely in place, to the longer of the time to clear the site and the switchover time.

A.3.3 Name Who Closes and Who Reopens, and Count to Full Flow

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-6. Name in advance the holders of the decisions to close and to reopen and to whom each passes when a holder cannot be reached; where your supply is divided, plan to reopen by part as each part’s supply and systems allow; and count recovery to the return of full traffic, including vehicles and crews out of position, not only to the return of power.

A.3.4 Plan With Your Utility

The review recommends, in its seventh recommendation, that critical sites plan for the loss of energy with a mechanism to bring the parties together. It also found that network operators’ license conditions generally prohibit treating one customer ahead of another in restoration, so a site must ride through the loss of any supply point on its own. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-5 and F-7: exchange with the utility, in writing, your supply points, the critical loads on each, your switchover time, and what each of you means by a resilient supply, renewed on change, and exercise the slowest switchover with the utility taking part.

A.4 For the Board

Just before midnight on 20 March 2025, a transformer caught fire at an electricity substation in west London. The fire took the substation’s other transformers with it, and with them one of the three points from which Heathrow Airport takes its power. The other two points kept their power, but each fed a separate part of the airport, and moving the lost point’s load onto them took manual switching through most of the next day. The airport closed for most of 21 March; about 1,300 flights were cancelled and some 200,000 to 300,000 passengers were disrupted, and full operation returned on 22 March, about twenty-eight hours after the loss. The review found the cause most likely to be moisture in a failed transformer component, and the police found no evidence that the incident was suspicious. The lesson does not depend on the cause. Three independent supplies are not redundancy unless each can carry the others’ critical loads in the time that matters, and that time is not known until it has been practiced. Any site fed from several points that each serve a separate part has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
