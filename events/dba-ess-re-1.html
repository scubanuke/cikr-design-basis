The 911 Outages After the Derecho of June 2012

Event date: 29 June to 3 July 2012 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

On the night of 29 June 2012 a derecho cut power across the Mid-Atlantic, and generators failed at two Verizon central offices in Northern Virginia, one of them untested under load for years. Fairfax County's 911 center, on its own generators, received no completed 911 call for seven hours, and nearly 1,900 calls were never routed to it. Across six states seventy-seven 911 centers serving more than 3.6 million people lost some service, and centers learned of it by the silence of their phones.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: a windstorm's loss of commercial power outlasting backup, and the failure of call delivery upstream of the centers, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, Detection of a silent failure must survive the loss of the power it watches. The carrier's own monitoring failed with the power, and centers found the outage themselves.
  • F-2, Backup power is credited only when it has been shown to run. Generators that had not been load-tested failed to start or carry the load.
  • F-3, Diversity is verified against the physical route. Circuits had single points of failure the records did not show.
  • F-4, The degraded states between silence and service are planned. Calls returned without the caller's number or location, and backup numbers failed too.
  • F-5, The area holds its own evidence of what lies upstream. 911 authorities should keep their own evidence of providers' measures, not rely only on certification.
  • F-6, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event was taken in by the class.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for 911 centers and the authorities that run them, and for the carriers and providers whose central offices, circuits, and monitoring deliver their calls. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose critical service depends on equipment upstream that it does not own. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know Your Backup Power Will Run

In December 2013 the Federal Communications Commission required covered 911 service providers to take reasonable measures for the diversity of 911 circuits, backup power at the central offices that directly serve 911 centers, and diverse network monitoring, and to certify them each year; in June 2026 it decided to replace the annual certification with a one-time certification updated on material change. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-2: credit backup power, at your center and upstream, only when it has been shown to run under load, recently.

A.3.2 Check Diversity on the Ground, and Hold Your Own Evidence

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-5. Verify that circuits recorded as diverse take different physical routes, and keep your own evidence of what your providers have done upstream, all the more now that certification is to be made once and updated on change.

A.3.3 Detect Silence, Reach the Carrier, and Plan the Degraded States

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-4. Make sure the detection of failed call delivery survives the loss of the power it watches; keep a way to reach your providers that does not depend on the failed network; and plan for calls arriving without number or location, and for an alternate number for the public that does not fail with your 911 lines.

A.4 For the Board

On the night of 29 June 2012 a fast-moving windstorm, a derecho, cut power across the Mid-Atlantic. In Northern Virginia, generators failed at two of Verizon’s central offices, one because it had not been tested under load in years, and 911 calls stopped reaching the county centers they served. Fairfax County’s 911 center, operating on its own generators, received no completed 911 call for seven hours, and nearly 1,900 calls were never routed to it. Neighboring centers found out by the silence of their phones and told the carrier, which had lost its own monitoring. Across six states seventy-seven 911 centers serving more than 3.6 million people lost some service. The Federal Communications Commission then required providers to keep diverse circuits, backup power, and monitoring. The lessons are that backup power counts only if it has been shown to run, that diversity must be checked on the ground, and that a center must be able to detect, and report, the silence of its own phones. Any operation whose critical service depends on equipment upstream that it does not own has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
