The Boeing 737 MAX Accidents and Grounding, 2018 to 2020

Event date: 29 October 2018, 10 March 2019, and the grounding to November 2020 and after · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

Lion Air Flight 610 on 29 October 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 on 10 March 2019 crashed shortly after takeoff, killing 346 people. In each, a flight-control function called MCAS acted repeatedly on a false reading from one angle-of-attack sensor and pushed the nose down, on a design whose pilots had not been told of the function. Regulators grounded the type worldwide over three days in March 2019; the United States returned it to service in November 2020 after a fix requiring both sensors, and other countries followed later.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: the investigating boards found the causes in the design, its certification, the documentation, maintenance, and the sensors, and no one suggests either crash was intended. It is an accidental fleet-wide design defect, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, The fleet shared the response to a fault, not the fault. The two sensor failures differed; what every airplane shared was the design, the missing alert, and manuals that did not name the function.
  • F-2, The crew was the design's second indication. The design relied on pilots to catch an error from a function they had not been told existed.
  • F-3, The range shown safe rested on the crew. A function shown safe only by counting on a human recovery has not been shown safe on its own.
  • F-4, The function grew before there was a fleet. The function was enlarged during development and not reassessed as a whole.
  • F-5, Fleet unavailability is counted by authority, and past delivery. The grounding ran longer and wider than the twenty months and 390 airplanes usually cited.
  • F-6, The decision to ground was held by many regulators on different bases. Each authority grounded and returned the type on its own basis, and each should record that basis.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators and maintainers of fleets whose units share automatic control functions, airlines first, and for the manufacturers and repair stations that supply them. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose equipment acts on its own on a sensor’s reading. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know What Your Automatic Functions Trust

For operators of the 737 MAX, the Federal Aviation Administration’s Airworthiness Directive 2020-24-02 requires, for each airplane, new flight-control and display software under which the function needs both angle-of-attack sensors to activate and acts only once per event, revised procedures, rerouted wiring, a sensor test, and a readiness flight, with the disagreement alert always enabled; and section 501 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023, requires further safety enhancements, among them a synthetic angle-of-attack system on the agency’s notice. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, and F-3, for any fleet: know which automatic functions command a control on one indication; name every such function in manuals and training; enable on every unit the alerts that would show an indication is wrong; and where safety rests on a person catching an error, show the time and indications assumed in the conditions the failure creates.

A.3.2 Watch Changes to Functions and the Parts That Feed Them

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2 and F-4. Ask that any change in development that enlarges a control function’s authority, or the conditions in which it acts, be reassessed as a whole function; know where the sensors that feed such functions are repaired and how they are tested on installation; and make sure that defects one crew reports reach maintenance and the next crew.

A.3.3 Ground on a Stated Basis, and Count the Whole Fleet

After the first accident, the agency issued an emergency directive requiring a revised crew procedure and kept the type in service; after the second, authorities grounded it over three days, each on its own basis. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-5 and F-6: state in advance the basis on which you would keep a type in service or ground it after a serious event, and who decides; and count a grounding by every authority that would ground your fleet, to the last unit returned and including units held from delivery.

A.4 For the Board

On 29 October 2018 and 10 March 2019, two new Boeing 737 MAX airliners crashed shortly after takeoff, in Indonesia and Ethiopia, killing 346 people. In each, a flight-control function called MCAS acted repeatedly on a false reading from a single angle-of-attack sensor and pushed the nose down. The pilots had not been told the function existed, and on most of the airplanes the alert that would have shown the two sensors disagreeing did not work. After the first crash the type stayed in service on a revised crew procedure; after the second, regulators around the world grounded it over three days, each on its own basis. The fix, which made the function use both sensors and act only once, took twenty months to certify in the United States, each airplane then needed its own modification, and other countries returned the type later still. The investigators found the causes in the design, its certification, the documentation, maintenance, and the sensors, and no one suggests either crash was intended. The lessons are that an automatic function that trusts one indication, and a safety case that counts on people catching an error they cannot see, will fail in every unit at once, and that the decision to keep a fleet in service after one accident must rest on a stated basis. Any fleet whose units share automatic functions has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
