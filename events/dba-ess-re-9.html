Air-Raid Alerting in Ukraine, 2022 to 2026

Event date: 24 February 2022 to 2026 · Read from instrument version 0.3 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

Since the full-scale Russian invasion of 24 February 2022, Ukraine has warned its population of air attack many thousands of times, by sirens, broadcast, messaging channels, a public application, and cell broadcast. The Air Force declares the threat and regional and district authorities warn their areas, and the signal between them was cut from minutes to seconds. When an attack on the largest mobile carrier took the alert system out in some areas in December 2023, the authorities said so and police and rescuers warned people by loudspeaker.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Military action by a state against a country, sustained for more than four years; the warning held under attack.

Findings

  • F-1, The signal from the authority that detects is part of the warning time. The signal from the Air Force to the regions took minutes until 2025.
  • F-2, A true alert that reaches too far costs response. Region-wide alerts for threats to a district wore down people's response.
  • F-3, The independence of a channel reaches its trigger. The alert system went out where it depended on the carrier that was attacked.
  • F-4, The authority knew the channel was down, and said so. Authorities announced the outage and named the channel that replaced it.
  • F-5, Grade the instruction by kind of threat, and re-validate it against the adversary. Ukraine introduced separate alerts for drones and for missiles in 2026.
  • F-6, Withdrawn. Its substance on campaign duration is carried by DBA-ESS-RE-12 F-6.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the authorities that warn the public of attack or other hazard, and for the operators of sirens, broadcast, cell broadcast, and alerting applications. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose warnings must keep reaching people while power and networks are under attack. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Shorten the Signal, and Match the Reach

Ukraine’s government reported cutting the signal from the Air Force Command to the regional authorities from several minutes to eight to fifteen seconds, divided warning below the region from January 2025 so that an alert sounds in the districts a threat approaches, and extended district alerting to every region but Donetsk and Luhansk in December 2025. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1 and F-2: count the signal from the authority that detects to the authority that warns as part of the warning time; and match the reach of each true alert to the area actually threatened, since alerts that reach too far wear down response.

A.3.2 Make the Trigger Independent, and Say When a Channel Is Down

When the attack on Kyivstar took the alert system out in Sumy on 12 December 2023, the city’s administration announced the outage and that police and rescue services would warn people. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3 and F-4: make each warning channel independent of the networks an attack would take, its trigger included; and when a channel is down, tell the public so and name what replaces it.

A.3.3 Grade the Warning by Kind of Threat

Ukraine announced in September 2026 a yellow alert for drones and a red one for missiles and massed attack, with criteria set by the military and civil authorities and reviewed weekly. This step adds the instrument’s own, from F-5: grade the instruction by the kinds of threat your area faces, and review it as the threat changes.

A.4 For the Board

Since the full-scale Russian invasion of 24 February 2022, Ukraine has warned its population of air attack many thousands of times, by sirens, broadcast, messaging channels, a public application, and cell broadcast. The Air Force declares the threat and regional and, since 2025, district authorities warn their areas, and the signal between them was cut from minutes to seconds. Region-wide alerts for threats to a single district wore down people’s response until warning was divided by district. When an attack on the country’s largest mobile carrier took the alert system out in some areas, the authorities said so and police and rescuers warned people with loudspeakers. In 2026 Ukraine began grading alerts by kind of threat. The warning has held under sustained attack. The lessons are that every second between detecting a threat and warning people counts, that a warning that reaches too far loses its audience, and that a channel’s independence must reach its trigger. Any authority that must warn people while power and networks are under attack has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
