Emergency Services · Reference event · DBA-ESS-RE-3

The 911 Call Flood of October 2016

Event date: 24 to 26 October 2016 · Read from instrument version 0.5 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

In late October 2016 a link posted to social media made iPhones dial 911 again and again without their users meaning to. One 911 center west of Phoenix received more than a hundred hang-up calls within minutes and was said to be in immediate danger of losing service, and calls reached centers in at least a dozen states. The flood eased when the page and the account were taken down at the police's request, and the phone's maker closed the route five months later.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate act, not established in its intent and not military action: a flood of calls beyond a center's capacity, induced through ordinary phones.

Findings

  • F-1, The flood began at a device that could call without its user, and was closed there. The defense lay where the call begins, outside the 911 system.
  • F-2, A generated flood is recognized by behavior, not identity. The calls came from many real phones and could be told apart only by how they behaved.
  • F-3, Stopping the source is part of the design duration, and belongs to others. The flood lasted until a hosting company and a platform acted.
  • F-4, An induced flood is measured by its distribution. The flood's reach was the link's audience, not a number of devices.
  • F-5, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.
  • F-6, A human act at the point of origin is a safeguard common to two events. Like the false alert in Hawaii, the remedy was a confirmation before the act.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for 911 centers and the authorities that run them, and for the makers of phones and the platforms whose links can cause devices to call. It is also for any owner, in any sector, whose service must answer every request and cannot refuse one. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Defend Where the Call Begins

No authority has published an action for 911 centers from this event; the route was closed at the handset by its maker five months later. This step is this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-6: the most effective defense against a generated flood lies where the call begins, in a device that asks its user before placing a call from a link, and 911 authorities should press for that safeguard, as they did for the confirmation of alerts after the false alert in Hawaii.

A.3.2 Recognize the Flood by Behavior

This step is this instrument’s, from F-2. Train and equip your center to recognize a generated flood by the behavior of the calls rather than their numbers, and plan what you will do when calls approach the level at which you would lose service, since you may not refuse them.

A.3.3 Know Who Can Stop the Source, and Watch the Distribution

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-3 and F-4. Know in advance which hosting companies, platforms, and device makers could stop a source, and how to reach them quickly; and count the reach of an induced flood by the reach of whatever is inducing it, so that centers where its audience is dense can prepare.

A.4 For the Board

In late October 2016 a link was posted to social media that made iPhones dial 911 again and again without their users meaning to. On the night of 25 October the 911 center of the Surprise Police Department, west of Phoenix, received more than a hundred hang-up calls within minutes, and the Maricopa County Sheriff’s Office said the center was in immediate danger of losing service; calls reached centers in at least a dozen states. No center could stop the flood: a 911 center cannot refuse a call, and the calls came from real phones. The flood eased when, at the police’s request, the hosting company removed the page and the account was shut down, and the route was closed at the handset by its maker five months later. The lessons are that a flood can be started by devices calling on their own, that it is recognized by its behavior rather than its numbers, and that the defense lies where the call begins. Any service that must answer every request has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
