The Denial of Large Transformer Replacement in Ukraine, 2022 Onward

Event date: March 2022 onward · Read from instrument version 0.6 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

From October 2022 Ukraine's power system was struck in repeated waves, in a campaign the International Criminal Court's warrants cover and that the court, Ukraine, and its partners attribute to the Russian armed forces, with large autotransformers named as key targets. By January 2023 the transmission operator said it needed fifty-nine; each can take up to two years to buy and deliver, and Europe's largest transformer plant, in Zaporizhzhia, had closed. Substations were struck again and again, and later strikes fell on the crews restoring them. The first donor-funded replacements arrived in October 2023.

Tier

Military-action tier met. Military action by a state's armed forces against the installed transformers and their replacement path together, sustained for years.

Findings

  • F-1, Repair capacity is part of the replacement path. More than half of the damaged units were struck repeatedly, which blocked their repair.
  • F-2, The emergency order path includes the buyer and the funds. The first replacements came through a United Nations agency acting as buyer with donor funds.
  • F-3, Each replacement should leave the adversary less to take. Replacements put back where the originals stood were struck again; each should move the grid toward a topology nearer the loads, toward microgrids, and over more corridors at voltage classes whose transformers are easier to source.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for electric utilities and transmission operators that depend on large power transformers, for the manufacturers and repair shops that build and mend them, and for the governments and agencies that would buy them in an emergency. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Plan Repair and the Emergency Order

No authority has published an action for owners that this instrument reads. These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2: make repair capacity part of the replacement path; and arrange in advance who buys a large transformer in an emergency and with what funds, as the United Nations Development Programme did for Ukraine’s transmission operator with funds from Japan.

A.3.2 Hold a Reserve Beyond the Campaign’s Reach

These steps are this instrument’s, drawn from the facility class’s governing scenario as the event characterizes it. Count the transformers a campaign could take and the reserve that would answer it; and hold part of the reserve beyond the campaign’s reach, as Ukraine’s foreign-built units were to be held in Poland and Romania until needed.

A.3.3 Protect the Restoration and Place the Replacement

Ukraine’s Ministry of Energy has withdrawn repair crews for a time where the risk of repeated attacks made the work unsafe, as it stated on 8 November 2025, and the practices Ukraine’s State Emergency Service adopted against second strikes on its rescuers are recorded in Repeat Strikes on Rescue Crews in Ukraine. UA Grid Defense: Cyber-Kinetic (Eclectic Technologies) recommends that each replacement be placed as close to the critical loads as transmission permits, along load paths put in priority in advance, and planned to reduce the megawatts lost per weapon. Each is its publisher’s. The remaining steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-3: plan restoration on the assumption that the crew and the unit may be struck again, and protect them before crediting them; and decide, before a loss, where and at what specification each critical transformer would be replaced, so that its replacement can leave the adversary less to take.

A.4 For the Board

From October 2022 Ukraine’s power system was struck in repeated waves, in a campaign the International Criminal Court’s warrants cover and that the court, Ukraine, and its partners attribute to the Russian armed forces. Large autotransformers were named as key targets. By January 2023 the transmission operator said it needed fifty-nine of them; each costs about $2 million, weighs hundreds of tons, and can take up to two years to buy and deliver. Europe’s largest transformer plant, in Zaporizhzhia, had closed in March 2022, and later attacks on Ukrainian factories slowed domestic production. More than half of the damaged units were struck more than once, which blocked their repair, and later strikes fell on the crews sent to repair them. The first new autotransformers bought with donor funds arrived in October 2023, through a United Nations agency acting as buyer. The lessons are that repair capacity is part of the replacement path, that someone must be ready to buy in an emergency, that a reserve must be held beyond the reach of the attack, and that a replacement put back where the original stood is put back where the attacker already knows to strike. Any utility that depends on large transformers has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this utility does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
