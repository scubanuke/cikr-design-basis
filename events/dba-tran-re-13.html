The Lac-Mégantic Runaway and Derailment, July 2013

Event date: 6 July 2013 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

Late on 5 July 2013, a freight train of seventy-two tank cars of crude oil was left unattended on a descending main line at Nantes, Quebec, held by seven hand brakes and by air from one running locomotive. After a fire, the locomotive was shut down, its air leaked away too slowly to set off the automatic brakes, and the train ran 7.2 miles downhill and derailed at 65 miles an hour in the center of Lac-Mégantic. The crude oil burned, and forty-seven people were killed.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: the Transportation Safety Board of Canada found no evidence of unauthorized entry to the train. It is an accidental loss of securement of an unattended train, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, The securement margin is a margin of force. The hand brakes gave about a third of the force needed because they were counted as brakes, not as force.
  • F-2, The test of securement is an indication. Securement was tested with other brakes applied, and the report built on that test was believed.
  • F-3, The automatic air brake is not fail-safe on a slow loss. The air leaked too slowly to set off the brakes, which were assumed to apply whenever air is lost.
  • F-4, The independent means, by where a runaway would go. The train was left with no derail or other independent defense, above a town.
  • F-5, An emergency act on a locomotive is an act on the train's securement. Shutting down the burning locomotive removed the air that partly held the train.
  • F-6, The runaway's margin begins at its first movement, and no one knew. The train ran for about seventeen minutes with nothing to stop it and no one aware it had gone.
  • F-7, A fleet shares configurations and practices, and may be answered by schedule. Retrofits and crew practices were shared across the fleet, and the tank cars were phased out by schedule rather than grounded.
  • F-8, The unattended train is a conveyance that can be taken. A train left unattended with an open cab should be protected as a vehicle that could be misused.
  • F-9, The line with the Chemical series and the crew question hold. The design basis divides the vehicle from its cargo correctly, and the Board could not conclude whether a one-person crew contributed.
  • F-10, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for railroads and the shippers of hazardous cargo by rail, and for the communities and responders along their lines. It is also for any owner, in any sector, of heavy vehicles or equipment left unattended where gravity can move them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Count and Test Securement as Force

The Federal Railroad Administration’s rule of 2015 requires hand brakes sufficient to hold the train with the air brakes released, applied and verified, and Transport Canada’s strengthened Rule 112 has required additional physical means of securement since 15 October 2015. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, and F-7: count securement as the force each applied brake gives, at its measured ratio and at the torque people actually apply, and credit a brake not shown working at nothing; release every air brake for the test and say in the report how the test was made; and inspect retrofitted protective functions for what they do, not for whether they are fitted.

A.3.2 Leave Trains Where a Runaway Cannot Reach a Town

Transport Canada’s emergency directive of 23 July 2013 prohibited leaving a locomotive coupled to loaded dangerous-goods tank cars unattended on a main track, and the Federal Railroad Administration’s rule requires a plan for where hazardous trains may be left unattended outside a yard. In March 2026 the Transportation Safety Board of Canada found that “a residual risk remains for uncontrolled movements in areas where derails do not protect the mainline.” These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3 and F-4: choose where a hazardous train may be left by where a runaway from there would go, preferring a place protected by a derail and using an independent means where there is none; and fit brakes that apply on a slow loss of air or on movement, by a means no shutdown, breaker, or setting can defeat.

A.3.3 Treat Any Emergency Act as an Act on Securement, and Know When a Train Moves

Transport Canada’s directive required unattended controlling locomotives to be protected against entry with their reversers removed, and the Federal Railroad Administration’s rule requires a locked cab and a qualified person to verify securement after emergency responders have been on the equipment. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-5, F-6, and F-8: tell responders what shutting down a locomotive takes away, and send someone qualified to restore securement before they leave; and make an unattended train detect its own movement, stop itself, report it, and warn what lies in its path.

A.4 For the Board

Late on 5 July 2013, a freight train of seventy-two tank cars of crude oil was left for the night on a descending main line at Nantes, Quebec, held by seven hand brakes and by air from one running locomotive that was already giving trouble. The locomotive caught fire, and firefighters shut it down, as instructed; with it went the air. The hand brakes gave about a third of the force needed, and as the air leaked away, too slowly to set off the automatic brakes, the train began to roll. It ran 7.2 miles downhill, reached 65 miles an hour, and derailed in the center of Lac-Mégantic. The crude oil burned, and forty-seven people were killed. The railway did not know its train had gone until the police called. The Transportation Safety Board of Canada found that the securement relied on the running locomotive, that the brakes were counted and tested wrongly, and that no physical defense stood in the way; it found no evidence that anyone had entered the train. The lessons are that what holds a heavy vehicle on a grade must be counted as force and tested in the condition it is relied on, that a fail-safe brake must act on a slow loss as well as a fast one, and that any act on a vehicle in an emergency is an act on what holds it in place. Any operation that leaves heavy equipment where gravity can move it has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
