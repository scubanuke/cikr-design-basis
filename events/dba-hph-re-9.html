The Release of Foot-and-Mouth Disease Virus From the Pirbright Site, 2007

Event date: August to November 2007 (record closed) · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Public Safety and Government collection

Impacted sector: Food and Agriculture

What happened

In August 2007 foot-and-mouth disease was confirmed on a farm near Guildford, and the strain was traced beyond reasonable doubt to the nearby Pirbright site, shared by a government institute and a vaccine maker. The Health and Safety Executive found live virus had most likely entered the site's cracked and root-damaged drains, that heavy rain may have increased the release, and that vehicle and people movements had not been adequately controlled; it could not say which occupant was the source.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: a decayed drain. None of the four characteristics is present.

Findings

  • F-1, The Release Was Found by the Disease It Caused. What leaves a high-containment site is monitored by each route.
  • F-2, Two Occupants Shared One Barrier, and No One Owned Its Condition. A shared barrier has one named owner.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for operators of high-containment laboratories and of facilities that make vaccines or work with dangerous agents, the regulators who license them, and the animal-health and public-health authorities near them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the facility class; this annex is addressed to a laboratory. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions a laboratory answers about its own site (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account a laboratory can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one laboratory. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the laboratory; it is that laboratory’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Follow the Guidance Already Published

The Health and Safety Executive’s final report of September 2007 set out the failures it found at the site, among them the condition of the drains and the control of movements, and the regulation of work with animal pathogens in Great Britain was reviewed afterwards. The class’s requirements (§4.6, §5.6) state barriers for each route, passive barriers maintained and inspected, control of movement, and a plan for a suspected release. Each is its author’s.

A.3.2 Watch What Leaves, and Give Each Shared Barrier One Owner

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-1 and F-2. Monitor what leaves your site by each route, effluent above all, for the agents you hold, so that a breach is found by you and not by a farmer. And if you share a site, name one owner for each shared barrier’s condition, inspection, and repair.

A.4 For the Board

In August 2007 foot-and-mouth disease broke out in cattle on a farm near Guildford in England. The government traced the virus to the nearby Pirbright site, where a government institute and a vaccine company worked with it. The Health and Safety Executive found that live virus had most likely leaked into the site’s drains, which were cracked and in poor condition, that heavy rain may have helped carry it out, and that vehicles and people moving near the drains had not been properly controlled. It could not say which of the two occupants was the source. Animals had to be culled and movements restricted. The lessons are that a laboratory’s oldest, simplest barriers need as much care as its newest, that a site should watch what leaves it rather than learn of a leak from a farmer, and that every shared barrier needs one owner. The questions in this annex show management whether the same exposure is present in its own site, and most of the answers cost little to find.
