The Bombings at Brussels Airport and Maalbeek Station, March 2016

Event date: 22 March 2016 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

On the morning of 22 March 2016, two bombs exploded in the public departure hall of Brussels Airport, before any screening, and about an hour later a third exploded in a metro train at Maalbeek station. Thirty-two people were killed and 340 injured on the parliamentary commission's count a week later. Between the two attacks the government decided to close the metro, but the decision reached the transport company only after the second explosion. The airport reopened in stages over the following three months, and the metro by station over the following month.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A terrorist attack by a non-state group, claimed for the Islamic State, as the group's claim; adversarial but not military action, since no state stood behind it on any account in the record. A Belgian court reached its verdicts in 2023.

Findings

  • F-1, The crowd outside the protected area, including the crowd the response makes. The attacks fell where crowds gathered before screening, and the reopening's screening outside the building formed new crowds in the open.
  • F-2, An attack on one node is a warning to the next. The decision to close the metro took thirty-one minutes to reach its operator, and the time from an outside decision to the start of clearance should be stated and measured.
  • F-3, The public authority closes the node, and the operator does not wait. Several public authorities can close a station, and the operator's plan should name each one and act without waiting on them.
  • F-4, The attack case, with the responders' way in, belongs in the node's plan. The airport's emergency plan had no part for an attack.
  • F-5, A separate channel that saturated. The emergency radio network saturated under load, so a backup channel must be sized and exercised for the event it is meant for.
  • F-6, An occupancy event strands flow, and the reopening regime sets the rate. The airport reopened at about a fifth of its capacity, and recovery runs to the end of the security regime it reopens under.
  • F-7, The adversary's sequence: the next node, during the response. The design basis should consider attacks on several nodes in sequence, and on the response itself.
  • F-8, The record measures no clearance time. The event confirms the need to clear crowds but supplies no measured time to do it.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of airports, rail and metro stations, bus terminals, and other passenger nodes, and for the public authorities that may close them. It is also for any owner, in any sector, of a public space where crowds gather before any screening. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know Where Your Crowds Form, Including the Ones Your Response Makes

This step is this instrument’s, from F-1. State where your processes gather crowds outside protected areas and hold them below a stated size by design. Before relying on a new security measure, state the crowd it forms, where, and for how long; on reopening, Brussels Airport’s screening before the building made queues of two to three hours outside it.

A.3.2 Make the Decision to Close Reach You Directly

The Belgian parliamentary commission of inquiry recommended that the federal Crisis Center’s management cell communicate its decisions directly to every service concerned, and that the rules on closing public transport say who decides and how the decision is communicated directly; the Brussels transport company’s plan now provides for direct notice. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-2 and F-3: measure the time from an outside authority’s decision to close to the start of your clearance, and keep it, with your clearance time, inside the interval your plan allows; name in your plan every authority that may close the site; and act on your own judgment without waiting for that order.

A.3.3 Plan and Exercise the Attack Case

The commission asked that the airport’s emergency and intervention plan be given a part on terrorism, with traffic management so that responders could reach and leave the scene, and that its evacuation plans be updated and exercised. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-4, F-5, and F-8: size and exercise your emergency channels at the load of the event, with the discipline of their use part of the exercise; treat your first information to responders as unverified until it is confirmed; and measure your clearance time in exercises.

A.3.4 Plan the Reopening

This step is this instrument’s, from F-6. State the rate you could sustain under the security regime you would impose after an attack, measure it in exercises, and count recovery to the end of that regime; Brussels Airport reopened at about a fifth of its capacity and was fully operational only in June.

A.4 For the Board

On the morning of 22 March 2016, two bombs exploded in the public departure hall of Brussels Airport, before any security screening, and about an hour later a third exploded in a metro train at Maalbeek station in central Brussels. Thirty-two people were killed and 340 injured on the parliamentary commission’s count a week later. The attacks were claimed for the Islamic State. Between the two explosions the government decided to close the metro, but the decision reached the transport company only after the second; the company had already begun stopping trains on its own. The airport’s emergency plan had no part for an attack, and the emergency radio network saturated. When the airport reopened, screening outside the building made long queues in the open, a new crowd outside any protection. The lessons for an operator are not about the attackers. Crowds gather where nothing protects them, including the crowds our own security measures create; a decision to close is only as fast as the channel that carries it; and a plan that has not imagined the case will not hold when it comes. Any site where the public gathers has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
