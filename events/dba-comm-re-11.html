The Galileo Outage, July 2019

Event date: 11 to 18 July 2019 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Lifelines collection

What happened

From 11 July 2019, the European Union's Galileo satellite navigation system stopped renewing the data its satellites broadcast, after a failure in its ground infrastructure during an upgrade, while its backup was temporarily limited. The satellites kept transmitting aging data, and receivers that checked its age excluded Galileo and, where they could, went on using other systems. Service returned with instability after about six days and fully after about eleven and a half.

Tier

No adversary established. Not a deliberate act: a failure of a constellation's ground segment during an upgrade, below the threshold for the military-action tier.

Findings

  • F-1, Withdrawn. Its correction of the facility class's account of the event went to the class.
  • F-2, A constellation's loss arrives as aging data under flags that did not at first mark it. The data aged while the satellites still reported themselves healthy, and only receivers that checked the age refused it.
  • F-3, The second constellation was independent against this failure. Receivers that combined Galileo with other systems kept working.
  • F-4, Redundancy out for maintenance is not redundancy. The failure came while the backup was limited for an upgrade.
  • F-5, The receiver design basis should carry what decided the event. Receivers should use several constellations and refuse data past its validity.
  • F-6, About six days is a point on the record. The outage gives a measured duration for the loss of a constellation.
  • F-7, Withdrawn. Its substance is carried by DBA-COMM-RE-10, The GPS Timing Error.
  • F-8, A foreign constellation is outside every series. A system run by another government lies outside anything this design basis can protect.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the operators of telecommunications networks, power grids, broadcasters, and other systems that use satellite navigation for position or time, and for the makers and integrators of their receivers. It is also for any owner, in any sector, who relies on a service operated by another government. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Refuse Aged Data, and Use More Than One Constellation

These steps are this instrument’s, from F-2, F-3, and F-5. Configure every receiver of consequence to refuse navigation data beyond its validity period, and to exclude a constellation whose data has aged, whatever its health flags say; configure receivers that can use several constellations to use them; and count another constellation as a second source against a constellation failure, but not against a jammer that covers both.

A.3.2 Count Maintenance as Absence, and Plan for a Week

The Inquiry Board set up by the European Commission recommended, on the Commission’s account, among other things, prompt and structured communication with users. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-4, F-6, and F-8: count anything out of service for maintenance or upgrade as absent from your redundancy; plan to ride through about six days without a constellation, and about eleven and a half of degraded service; detect the loss by your own monitoring; and count reliance on a constellation operated by another government as a dependency no series of the design basis protects.

A.4 For the Board

From the early hours of 11 July 2019, the European Union’s satellite navigation system, Galileo, stopped renewing the orbit and clock data its satellites broadcast, after a failure in its ground infrastructure during an upgrade, at a time when the backup was temporarily limited. The satellites kept transmitting, but their data aged, and for days their health flags did not mark it as bad. Receivers that checked the data’s age excluded Galileo, and those that could use GPS or other systems went on working; a receiver restricted to Galileo had no fix, and a research receiver that skipped the age check was off by 500 meters or more. Service returned with instability after about six days, and fully after about eleven and a half. The operator’s first notice came about fourteen hours after the outage began, and the full inquiry report has not been published. No source suggests the failure was deliberate. The lessons are that a receiver must refuse data that has aged, that a second constellation protects against the failure of one, and that redundancy out for maintenance is not redundancy. Any operation that depends on satellite navigation has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
