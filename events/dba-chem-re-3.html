The Chlorine Release at Graniteville, January 2005

Event date: 6 January 2005 · Read from instrument version 0.4 · Industry and Economy collection

What happened

At about 2:39 a.m. on 6 January 2005, a freight train running through Graniteville, South Carolina, was diverted by a main-line switch a local crew had left lined for an industry track, and struck a standing train. A chlorine tank car was punctured and about 120,000 pounds of chlorine escaped within minutes into a valley holding houses and a mill running a night shift. Nine people died, among them six mill workers and the train's engineer.

Tier

No adversary established. Not an attack: the cause, on the National Transportation Safety Board's determination, was a switch left misaligned, and the switch showed no evidence of tampering.

Findings

  • F-1, Movement protection must know the switch. Nothing detected the switch's position, and the train could not stop in the distance its banner could be seen.
  • F-2, The movement authority rested on one person's memory. The train was cleared on a crew member's belief that the switch was restored.
  • F-3, The strongest car was punctured by its neighbor. A car among the strongest in service was punctured by another car's coupler.
  • F-4, A deliberate diversion needs no weapon. The same consequence could be reached on purpose through an unmonitored switch.
  • F-5, The ground, the night shift, and the building's air set the exposure. The cloud settled in the valley and was drawn into the mill by its ventilation.
  • F-6, The first decisions are made before the cargo is known. Responders learned what the train carried only after their first decisions.
  • F-7, The crew and the trackside facility stood in the footprint. The engineer survived the collision and died in the cloud.
  • F-8, Who held each decision on the cargo is not on the record. Twelve days of handling the damaged car included a failed patch and a second release.
  • F-9, Withdrawn. Its qualification of the facility class's account of the event went to the class as an erratum.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for railroads that carry inhalation-hazard chemicals, the shippers that load them, and the plants and communities that stand beside the track. It is also for the fire departments and emergency managers who would answer a release. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Know the Switch, Independently of the Crew

The National Transportation Safety Board did not believe rules and forms were sufficient to prevent such errors, and recommended a device conveying switch status independent of the switch banner and train speeds that allow a stop short of a misaligned switch. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1, F-2, and F-4: on lines carrying inhalation-hazard cars, give the movement authority an independent indication of switch position; and treat a hand-operated switch in such territory as a deliberate route to release, not only an accidental one.

A.3.2 Place the Cars, and Prepare the Neighbors

The Board found that placing such cars toward the rear of a train would reduce their exposure in a derailment. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-3 and F-5: place inhalation-hazard cars where they are least exposed; and make sure plants beside the track can stop their ventilation and shelter their workers, the night shift especially.

A.3.3 Equip the Crew, Inform the Responders, and Name the Holders

The federal rule of 2024 now requires escape breathing apparatus for train crews carrying such cargo. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-6, F-7, and F-8: give responders the train’s cargo within minutes; prepare first decisions that can be made before the cargo is known; and name who holds each decision in the days of handling a damaged car.

A.4 For the Board

At about 2:39 a.m. on 6 January 2005, a Norfolk Southern freight train running through Graniteville, South Carolina, was diverted by a main-line switch that a local crew had left lined for a mill’s industry track the evening before, and struck a standing train. A chlorine tank car, among the strongest in service, was punctured by the coupler of another car, and about 120,000 pounds of chlorine escaped within minutes into a valley holding houses and a mill that ran through the night. Nine people died, among them six mill workers and the train’s engineer, and thousands were evacuated. Nothing reported the switch’s position; the train could not stop in the distance at which the switch’s banner could be seen; and responders learned what the train carried only after their first decisions. The National Transportation Safety Board, whose report is the record, found that rules and forms alone were not enough. The lessons are that on lines carrying such cargo the position of a switch must be known independently of the crew, and that the plants and responders beside the track must be ready for a cloud. Any railroad or plant along a hazardous-materials route has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show management whether this operation does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
