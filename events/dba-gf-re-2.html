The Breach of the United States Capitol, January 2021

Event date: 6 and 7 January 2021 · Read from instrument version 0.2 · Public Safety and Government collection

What happened

On 6 January 2021, during the joint session of Congress to count the electoral votes, a crowd overcame the police lines and entered the Capitol. The Senate recessed at 2:13 p.m., floor staff carried the states' certificates from the chamber, and about 140 officers were assaulted. The National Guard began arriving at 5:20 p.m., about three hours after the request was approved, and the count was completed in the night.

Tier

Adversarial, not military action. A deliberate adversarial event, not military action: no state is alleged in any account to have acted.

Findings

  • F-1, The reinforcement time is the sum of two chains. Approval of the request and the Guard's arrival each took time.
  • F-2, The instruments of a time-bound act are records. The count rested on paper certificates carried from the floor.

For asset owners

Annex A of the instrument, in full. It is written for owners and operators, and it states no requirement.

A.1 What This Annex Is

This annex is for the security leaders of legislatures, courts, election offices, and other government bodies that must complete acts scheduled by law, and for the police and military forces that would reinforce them. It is not a finding and states no requirement. The recommendations of Section 8 are addressed to the framework; this annex is addressed to an operator. It has three parts, each drawn from a section of the instrument: a self-check that turns the event’s elements (Table 4-1) into questions an operator answers about the operator’s own system (A.2); the actions published, each stated as its publisher’s, with this instrument’s own marked and tied to a finding (A.3); and a plain-language account an operator can give a board (A.4).

A.2 Would It Happen Here?

The instrument reads the event against the design basis. The self-check reads the same event against one operation. Each question is keyed to the element of Table 4-1 it comes from. A “no” or an “I don’t know” is not a failure of the operator; it is that operation’s finding, and the place to start.

A.3 What to Do Now

A.3.1 Plan Each Time-Bound Act

The Senate committees’ bipartisan report found failures of intelligence and of planning, found that the Capitol Police prepared no department-wide operational or staffing plan for the joint session and that its leadership did not give front-line officers effective protective equipment or training, and recommended that the Chief be empowered to request the National Guard’s assistance in an emergency. No step of this instrument’s is added here.

A.3.2 Shorten Reinforcement, and Protect the Act’s Records

Public Law 117-77, of 22 December 2021, allows the Chief of the Capitol Police to request assistance without the Board’s prior approval when the Chief determines it necessary. These steps add the instrument’s own, from F-1 and F-2: count reinforcement time as the sum of the time to approve the request and the time for the reinforcing force to arrive; and treat the physical instruments of a time-bound act as records, protected so the act can resume.

A.4 For the Board

On 6 January 2021 Congress met in joint session to count the electoral votes of the 2020 presidential election, an act scheduled by the Constitution and by law. A crowd overcame the police lines and entered the Capitol; the Senate went into recess at 2:13 p.m., floor staff carried the states’ certificates from the chamber, and about 140 officers were assaulted. The Capitol Police Board approved a request for the National Guard at 2:10 p.m., and the Guard began arriving at 5:20 p.m. The Senate returned at 8:06 p.m., and the count was completed in the night. A bipartisan Senate report found failures of intelligence and planning, no department-wide plan for the session, and officers without the equipment or training they needed; Congress later let the Chief of the Capitol Police request help without the Board’s prior approval. The House select committee’s findings on the event’s cause are its own. The lessons are that an act the law schedules must be planned for as the most exposed day of the year, that reinforcement time counts both the approval and the arrival, and that the papers of the act are records to protect. Any body that must complete an act by a date set in law has the same exposure. The questions in this annex show leadership whether this body does, and most of the answers cost little to find.
